## CHAPTER 1 — How to Read This Document

This is not a story with a villain and a hero. It is the complete record of one of the most contested criminal episodes in modern American history, assembled claim by claim, with every claim labeled by where it comes from. Before anything else, you need to know the rules this document follows, because the rules are what make it worth reading.

**The pedigree system.** Every factual claim falls into one of three grades:

- **Grade A — proven fact.** Established by courts, government records, official documents, or contemporaneous reporting by major outlets. The grand jury's final report, the NCUA's annual report, appellate opinions, FBI documents, sworn testimony.
- **Grade B — documented but disputed.** A claim that appears in primary documents or credible testimony but is contested or unverified. Witness statements, affidavits, letters, interviews.
- **Grade C — claimed.** An allegation or theory from researchers, victims' advocates, books, or advocacy publications. Some of it is anchored in documents; some of it is not. It is recorded because even unproven claims shape events — but it is never treated as fact.

Where a name or a website has a history of spreading hatred or fabricated material, it is logged in a contamination register and its claims are excluded from the evidentiary record. That register is at the back. Every conflict between sources — and there are many — is logged in a conflict register rather than silently resolved. When this document says "on the record," it means a Grade A or B item. When it says "claimed," it means Grade C and the claim is treated as a claim.

**The three strata.** The Franklin case contains three different kinds of material that are constantly blurred together, to the confusion of everyone who touches it. Keeping them separate is the single most important discipline in reading this file:

1. **The documented crime layer.** Lawrence King ran a credit union and stole roughly $39 million from it — this is not in dispute. A foster home belonging to his cousin was found by the grand jury itself to have substantiated physical and sexual abuse. A newspaper columnist was convicted of molesting boys. A department-store executive pleaded to pandering. The money handlers paid $10 million to settle claims. None of this requires believing anything controversial.
2. **The contested testimony layer.** A group of young people said they were trafficked by prominent men. Their accounts were videotaped, then recanted, then un-recanted, then litigated. Two grand juries and a trial jury said the accounts were lies. The accusers' advocates say the recantations were coerced. This layer is genuinely unresolved, and this document presents it unresolved.
3. **The overlay layer.** Accounts of satanic rituals, murdered infants, mind control, CIA programs, and international networks. This is the layer where the 1990s "satanic panic" scholarship bites hardest, and where the most contaminated sources in the record concentrate. It is documented as claims, with its pedigree stated, and nothing more.

**The balance rule.** Where the official record says one thing and the critics say another, both are presented. Where an official was cleared, the clearing is stated. Where a clearing is criticized, the criticism is stated with its evidence. Nobody in this document is convicted of anything this document convicts them of; every adjudicated fact is attributed to the body that adjudicated it.

**Chronology.** The story is told in the order it happened, because cause and effect live in the order of events. Where the Epstein era (2019-2026) casts light backward onto Franklin, that light is brought in where it belongs in the narrative — and again, in full, in the dedicated bridge chapter near the end.

One more note on names. Several people in this story share names with others who have nothing to do with it — including a famous broadcaster, a Maryland sex offender, and a Minnesota farmer. Every time such a name appears, it has been identity-checked. The namesake register is in the appendix.

---

**A worked example.** Take one sentence from the case and watch the grades do their work. "The grand jury found that the allegations were a hoax." Grade A — the report says exactly that. "The hoax verdict rested on the recantations of Troy Boner and Danny King." Grade A — the report says that too. "Boner later swore he lied because the FBI gave him a choice between lying and dying." Grade B — a fourteen-page notarized affidavit, uncontradicted, never tested. "And the phone call Boner said Owen used to script him never happened." Grade B — the Caracorp phone records, produced at trial. Now stack them: the Grade A verdict stands; the Grade B items are the documented reasons a great many people, in Nebraska and far beyond, never accepted it. Neither stack cancels the other, and this document never asks them to.

---

**Plain words.** A reader should never need a law degree or a newspaper career to follow this file, so the working vocabulary, once:

- *Grand jury* — a panel of ordinary citizens a prosecutor convenes to decide whether evidence is strong enough to charge someone. It is not a trial; the accused has no lawyer there, and its rules are the prosecutor's.
- *Recantation* — taking back testimony. As Chapter 13 shows, it can mean the original story was false, or that pressure buried a true one; the word itself decides nothing.
- *Deposition* — sworn testimony taken outside a courtroom, on the record, usable later.
- *Default judgment* — what a court enters when one side simply never shows up or answers. It is not a finding after a fight; it is a win by forfeit.
- *Restitution* — money a convicted person is ordered to repay.
- *Receiver* — the court-appointed manager who takes over a failed institution's remains.
- *Correspondent bank* — the bank where a smaller institution holds its operating account; Franklin's was FirsTier.
- *Peremptory strike* — a lawyer's no-questions-asked removal of a would-be juror; each side is allowed only so many.
- *FOIA* — the Freedom of Information Act, the public's formal tool for prying documents out of the government.
- *Non-prosecution agreement (NPA)* — a deal in which the government agrees not to charge, usually in exchange for something; Epstein's 2008 NPA is the most contested such document in the modern record.
- *Discharge petition* — the House rule that forces a bill to a floor vote if 218 members sign it, bypassing the leadership; it is the legislative weapon of last resort, used twice in this story.
- *Mann Act* — the federal law against transporting a person across state lines for sex; the 1989 question was whether Franklin's flights triggered it.
- *Ponzi scheme* — fraud that pays old promises with new money; it works until the arithmetic turns.
- *Pandering* — the legal term for procuring people for prostitution.
- *Impaneled* — formally seated, as a jury is.
- *Foreman* — the juror the panel elects to speak for it.

## CHAPTER 2 — The Man and the Money

### 2.1 Lawrence Edward King Jr.

Lawrence E. "Larry" King Jr. was born September 7, 1944, in Omaha, Nebraska, the son of a small businessman. The surviving record of his life reads like a study in American reinvention. He ran errands at Omaha's Blackstone Hotel at fourteen and waited tables at sixteen, sang in Central High School musicals, studied voice at the University of Nebraska at Omaha, and earned a business degree in 1972. By twenty-six he was treasurer-manager of the Franklin Community Federal Credit Union, a small institution founded to serve the low-income Black community of north Omaha. [Grade A: his resume, preserved in the Reagan Presidential Library, confirms the dates.]

The resume itself is a document worth dwelling on, because it shows how completely King colonized every institution that touches children and families in a mid-sized American city. Girls Club of Omaha: president, 1973-1975; treasurer, 1975; regional president and then assistant secretary of the national Girls Clubs of America through 1981. Head Start: board of directors, 1974-1975. Camp Fire Girls: executive committee member, then president, 1979-1980. Youth Opportunities Unlimited: board, 1978. Lutheran Medical Center trustee. Girls Club again as it merged toward Boys & Girls Clubs. Planned Parenthood board member. Presbyterian church elder. Opera Omaha vice president; Omaha Ballet; the National Council of the Metropolitan Opera; an honorary master's degree from Cornell and an honorary doctorate from Hunter College, both bestowed in the late 1970s and early 1980s. [Grade A: Reagan Library WHORM file 40-819-198416.]

None of this is allegation. This is the official record. The man who would later be accused of sourcing children from institutions had spent fifteen years sitting on the boards of those institutions.

His rise in Republican politics was equally documented. He chaired the Nebraska Black Republican Council, became vice chairman for finance of the National Black Republican Council, chaired business coordinating and fundraising committees at the national level, sang the national anthem at the 1984 Republican National Convention in Dallas and again at the 1988 convention in New Orleans, served on the advisory committee for Ronald Reagan's second inauguration, and campaigned for Reagan across five states in 1984. The Reagan White House correspondence file includes a personal letter from King to a presidential assistant dated November 4, 1981, and a 1982 letter from a Franklin Credit Union development officer enclosing King's resume, which the White House forwarded to the Office of Presidential Personnel "with no particular recommendation." In March 1985, a member of Congress issued a news release recommending King for a seat on the National Endowment for the Arts advisory council, describing him as "a hand up, not a hand-out." [Grade A: Reagan Library documents.]

His lifestyle, paid for out of a credit-union salary that never exceeded roughly $16,200 a year, became the subject of federal scrutiny. In the thirteen months before the credit union closed, King charged $1,131,229 on six credit cards — over a million dollars of it on a single American Express account. He spent $186,395 on limousines, $45,806 on chartered airplanes, $45,166 at jewelers, and $145,057 at florists. He chartered aircraft almost weekly, favoring Washington, D.C. He maintained a $5,000-a-month rented house on Washington's Embassy Row. He threw a $100,000 party for a thousand friends at the 1988 Republican convention. [Grade A: court records; contemporaneous reporting; research by author Nick Bryant in *The Franklin Scandal* (2009).]

### 2.2 The Franklin Credit Union and the fraud

The Franklin Community Federal Credit Union was founded to serve north Omaha. By King's account it was "my baby," a unique institution helping low-income citizens. By the National Credit Union Administration's account, it was a looting operation concealed from examiners for years by what the agency's 1989 annual report called "a separate, elaborate computer program" that hid unrecorded certificate-of-deposit obligations. [Grade A: NCUA 1989 Annual Report, official document.]

The mechanism, as reconstructed by the federal regulator: Franklin sold CDs without recording the liabilities. The money was diverted to King's personal living and business expenses, to above-market dividends paid to keep existing depositors quiet, and to operating costs that had no connection to credit-union business. The institution skipped its mandatory annual audits from 1985 through 1988. The NCUA would later state plainly that had its auditors done their jobs, "King's misdeeds would have been discovered early enough to have prevented the collapse." [Grade A: NCUA report; the grand jury report's own criticism.]

There was a whistleblower. Edward Hobbs, a five-year Franklin teller, wrote a memo to the credit union's board in February 1984 alleging embezzlement. He was fired the next day. When State Senator Peter Hoagland carried his information to Banking Director Roger Beveridge, the answer was that nothing could be done, because action "would seem like the government was attacking a small credit union serving a primarily minority clientele." [Grade B: DeCamp's book records the exchange; the grand jury report confirms Hobbs was fired in 1984 for the memo and that the banking director told him nothing could be done.]

On November 4, 1988, federal agents raided and closed Franklin. The ledgers showed $2.6 million in liabilities; the hidden obligations totaled roughly $37 million. The NCUA's eventual loss figure was $39.2 million. [Grade A: NCUA report.]

King was indicted on forty federal counts in May 1989. What happened over the next two years is a study in delay. A magistrate ordered King to a federal psychiatric facility on February 7, 1990 — the morning of a presidential fundraising visit to Omaha. He was found incompetent, sent to a federal medical facility in Rochester, Minnesota, for roughly five months, and restored to competency about five weeks after the grand jury that would have examined him finished its work. The grand jury record states that it requested King to appear voluntarily and that he declined. He was never subpoenaed. [Grade A: court records; Grade A: the grand jury report's own text; Grade C: the timing relative to the Bush fundraiser is noted by researchers as suspicious but is circumstantial.]

In February 1991, King and his wife Alice pleaded guilty to conspiracy, embezzlement, and false statements. He agreed to pay $41.8 million in restitution; by mid-1993 he had paid $25. Alice served in Texas and was released in 1993. King was sentenced to fifteen years in August 1991 by Judge William Cambridge, served about ten at the federal prison camp in Florence, Colorado, and was released on April 10, 2001, to the eastern Virginia federal probation district — the Washington suburbs area where he had kept his second home in the 1980s. [Grade A: Omaha World-Herald, April 11, 2001; court records.]

He has said nothing publicly since. As of the latest verified contact attempt — a 2023 CNN inquiry — he declined to respond. No obituary for him has ever been located. [Grade A: CNN, 2023; obituary searches through 2026.]

### 2.3 The institutions around him

The credit union's governing board included King's cousin Jarrett Webb and a man named James C. Hart Jr. Its advisory board — the fundraising and respectability arm — was chaired by Harold Andersen, publisher of the Omaha World-Herald, the state's dominant newspaper, and included executives from ConAgra, Union Pacific, Mutual of Omaha, Northwestern Bell, and the city's major law firms and banks. Andersen personally chaired a 1984 fundraising drive that raised $672,170 for Franklin and, in 1986, helped raise $600,000 for a renovation that included a bedroom suite in the credit union's basement. [Grade A: advisory board roster, NCUA records; Grade B: a security guard who lived in the basement bedroom later alleged his first duty was sexual, per Bryant's research.]

Andersen's newspaper employed, as a gossip columnist, a man named Peter Citron — later convicted of sexually assaulting boys, with videotapes and computer discs seized at his arrest. The paper that would editorialize against the Franklin accusers thus employed a convicted child molester throughout the period. [Grade A: Citron's conviction and sentencing, July 1990.]

This is the documented world into which the allegations walked.

**The resume, in full.** The document the White House held is worth inventorying completely, because it shows the machine that one man can build inside a city's civic life: Girls Club of Omaha president 1973-75 and treasurer 1975; regional president of the Girls Clubs of America and its national assistant secretary through 1981; Camp Fire Girls executive committee 1974-78 and president 1979-80; Head Start board 1974-75; Youth Opportunities Unlimited 1978; the University of Nebraska at Omaha chancellor's advisory committee from 1979; Lutheran Medical Center trustee 1975-78; Opera Omaha second vice president; the National Black Republican Council's business coordinating committee chairman and campaign finance chairman; the Nebraska Frederick Douglass Republican Council; the National Council of the Metropolitan Opera; a member of the White House Conference on Families steering committee; elder of his church; member of the Omaha Club, the Omaha Press Club, the Racquet Club, the Ski Club. The awards column runs eleven lines, beginning with a Thrift Honor Award from the NCUA itself — in 1972, sixteen years before the agency closed him down. [Grade A: Reagan Library WHORM file.]

**The galas.** In 1987 King gave $25,350 to Citizens for America, the organization running Oliver North's speaking tours during the Iran-Contra period; the White House later denied any impropriety. At the 1988 Republican convention in New Orleans, King's Council of Minority Americans staged a $100,000 gala whose host committee included former President Gerald Ford, former Secretary of State Alexander Haig, and Congressman Jack Kemp; a videotape of Kemp and King urging the Black vote for Bush was screened. That same year, out of credit-union funds, King donated $18,000 to a Washington gay and lesbian rights group's camp fund. A four-foot photograph of King with Ronald Reagan greeted visitors to the credit union. And in September 1988, days after singing at the convention and weeks before the raid, King told a local magazine of his "adoring relationship" with the late CIA Director William Casey. [Grade A: campaign-finance records, contemporary profiles, the WHORM files; Grade B: the Casey remark.]

---

**The competency episode, in full.** On February 7, 1990 — the morning President Bush arrived in Omaha for a fundraiser for Governor Kay Orr — U.S. Magistrate Richard Kopf ordered King to the federal medical facility at Springfield, Missouri, without a motion from King or his attorneys, and sealed the psychiatric report. King's pastor and his flower-shop employer told the Lincoln Journal two days later that they had seen no sign of mental illness — "I would be surprised if King were declared unfit." The Springfield chief psychiatrist diagnosed a "probable delusional paranoid disorder — grandiose type," and Judge Cambridge then transferred King to the Rochester, Minnesota, facility as a pretrial detainee. He was restored to competency roughly five weeks after the grand jury's report issued, and returned to Omaha at the end of August 1990. The grand jury's report itself records "much disagreement" on the incompetency ruling among those who observed King and describes him as "a very clever and manipulative person" who may have inveigled himself into the hospital — in which case, as researchers have noted, the deception and complicity ran to two federal judges, neither of whom has ever been asked about that morning. King had bought a ticket to the Bush fundraiser; the Secret Service discovered his plan, and he was at the federal courthouse by early afternoon. [Grade A: the court orders and dates; Grade B: the employer and pastor; Grade C: the inference of choreography.]

## CHAPTER 3 — The Allegations Emerge (1985-1989)

### 3.1 The Webb home

The first allegations did not come from teenagers with criminal records. They came from the foster-care system itself.

Barbara Webb was Larry King's first cousin. She and her husband Jarrett Webb ran a foster home in Washington County, north of Omaha. Beginning in June 1985, boys in the home reported physical abuse; that November, two girls — sisters Lisa and Tracey — alleged abuse by Jarrett Webb, including sexual abuse. The children were not removed for roughly ten months. [Grade A: the grand jury's own report confirms every element of this paragraph, including the removal delay, which the jurors called "unfortunate."]

The grand jury's report, which would later brand the Franklin abuse story "a carefully crafted hoax," also stated this, in its own words: the initial allegations "appear to be substantiated by the existence of some marks on the bodies of the children and by the fact that Lisa passed a polygraph test administered by the Nebraska State Patrol." [Grade A: grand jury report, Exhibit A and Section IV.]

The older sister — whose legal name was Eulice Washington, also known as Lisa Washington Webb, and later in life Nelly Patterson — told foster parent Kathleen Sorenson and Boys Town youth worker Julie Walters that her foster parents took her, beginning at about age fifteen, on trips with Larry King to Chicago, New York, and Washington, D.C., where she was, in her account, used as a prostitute at parties. Walters produced a fifty-page handwritten report dated March 25, 1986. [Grade B: the report exists and its contents are documented; the allegations within it are testimony, not fact.]

The grand jury found probable cause to believe that on May 15 and June 1, 1985, Lisa had been subjected to sexual contact overcome by force or coercion, and that Jarrett Webb "committed the acts." Because the acts occurred in Washington County, outside the grand jury's jurisdiction, it could not indict; its interim report recommended the county attorney file two counts of third-degree sexual assault. [Grade A: Exhibit A of the grand jury report, in full.]

The county attorney did not file. In December 1990 — a year and a half after the grand jury's recommendation — a Washington County judge dismissed all charges against Jarrett and Barbara Webb, ruling the statute of limitations had run because the assaults occurred two days after Lisa's sixteenth birthday, disregarding Nebraska's tolling rule for underage victims. [Grade A: court record.]

The grand jury report named every official whose failure allowed this: Attorney General Robert Spire's office, which was told of "a possible child exploitation ring" in July 1988 and passed it on without timely action; the assistant attorney general who received it, whose "heavy workload" caused delays; Washington County Attorney Patrick Tripp, who "should have filed charges"; guardian ad litem Patricia Flocken, who said she was intimidated by the county attorney; Foster Care Review Board director Carol Stitt, whom the jury faulted for reporting to the attorney general "so promptly" without making more inquiries first; and the sheriff and county judge who "should have pressed." [Grade A: Section IV, in full.]

Read that again slowly, because it is the hinge of the entire case: the grand jury that called the Franklin story a hoax substantiated the abuse at its source node, named the men and women who failed the children, and recommended prosecution — while simultaneously concluding it could find no evidence that the children had been flown anywhere for illegal purposes. The hoax verdict and the substantiated-abuse verdict coexist in the same document.

### 3.2 Loretta Smith and the street-level reports

In 1988, a girl identified in the record as Loretta Smith — seventeen when she testified — came through Richard Young Hospital. Her accounts described abuse beginning around age nine: a man named "Ray" associated with the Girls Club of Omaha took her and other girls to parties with older men. Later, she described ritualized gatherings involving drugs, candles, and, at around age ten, being locked in a room where men killed an infant in front of her. She named Larry King and "Mr. Finch, a school principal" as participants in abuse, and described further killings of children in the spring of 1985, when she was eleven. She was hospitalized repeatedly and eventually placed at the Uta Halee girls' home. [Grade C for the killings and ritual content — the grand jury found her accounts of these specifics could not be corroborated and noted she herself acknowledged difficulty, later in life, distinguishing reality from fantasy due to drug use; Grade B for the abuse and party attendance, which the grand jury treated as credible in substance.]

Here is the documented spine: the Omaha Police interviewed her in June 1988. "No meaningful follow-up was accomplished," the grand jury found, "even though the girl told tales of possible homicides." She later attended parties hosted by Larry King and saw no drugs or illegal activity there. The grand jury's conclusion about her was the most double-edged sentence in its report: she "suffered more abuse and neglect than anyone should ever have to endure," her perpetrators "may never be known" — and "no evidence" connected King or anyone at Franklin to her abuse. [Grade A: grand jury report, Section IV.]

### 3.3 The Foster Care Review Board and the legislature

The Nebraska Foster Care Review Board — a statutory watchdog over the foster-care system — accumulated the early reports: the Webb allegations, the Smith accounts, and information from youth facilities. In July 1988, board director Carol Stitt forwarded the material to the attorney general's office with a warning that it suggested a possible child exploitation ring. The board's investigator, Jerry Lowe, wrote in February 1989 that "what appears to be documented cases of child abuse and sexual abuse dating back several years with no enforcement action taken by the appropriate agencies is... mind boggling." [Grade A: grand jury report; Lowe's memo.]

In December 1988, State Senator Ernie Chambers told colleagues he had received credible reports of "boys and girls, some of them from foster homes, who had been transported around the country by airplane to provide sexual favors, for which they were rewarded." The legislature voted unanimously in a special session to create a special committee, chaired by Senator Loran Schmit, with Chambers as vice-chair, to investigate the Franklin collapse. [Grade A: New York Times, December 18, 1988; legislative records.]

The committee hired counsel and an investigator. In July 1989 it fractured: Chambers, investigator Lowe, and counsel Kirk Naylor resigned on July 13, protesting Chairman Schmit's public statements that the committee had found no smoking gun. Schmit wanted to follow the money. New counsel, John Stevens Berry, was hired, and on August 21, 1989, the committee hired a private investigator named Gary Caradori. [Grade A: UPI, July 25, 1989; committee records.]

**The Walters report, in its own words.** Julie Walters' fifty-page report of March 25, 1986 survives, and its most quoted passage describes the Washington trips in the teenager's own account as Walters recorded it: "Lisa has accompanied Mr. and Mrs. King and Prince [King's son] on trips to Chicago, New York, and Washington, D.C., beginning when she was 15 years old... She missed 22 days of school almost totally due to these trips. Lisa was taken along on the pretense of being Prince's babysitter. Last year she met V.P. George Bush and saw him again at one of the parties Larry gave while on a Washington, D.C. trip. At some of the parties there are just men (as was the case at the party George Bush attended) older men and younger men in their early 20s: Lisa said she has seen sodomy committed at those parties." [Grade B: the report and its authentication are documented; its contents are testimony.]

Against that account stands the documented opportunity record and the absence record: Vice President Bush was in Illinois campaigning in late October 1984, corroborating the trip's timeframe but not the party; the grand jury found no witness could confirm Lisa's transport for illegal purposes; and the grand jury's own report records that several witnesses who traveled with King made "embarrassing admissions" of sexual contact with him on those trips. The Bush sighting remains, on the full record, an unadjudicated claim by a credible-appearing witness whose account of the abuse around it was substantiated in part by the grand jury itself — and a claim no proceeding ever tested. [Grade A/B analysis.]

**The early investigation's other records.** Senator Schmit met King in February 1989 — King name-dropped Andersen, FBI agents, and the Omaha Police. Attorney General Spire, testifying to the committee that June, said that "based on conclusions and reports of the FBI, the Omaha Police, and the Nebraska State Patrol, all three of those have concluded that the reports do not have substance" — the three agencies that had stood down, now cited as proof there was nothing to stand down for. [Grade A/B.]

---

**The Concerned Parents.** In July 1989, parents and foster parents organized as the Concerned Parents Group to press the investigation forward. Its front-line organizers paid for it. Bonnie Cosentino — whose grand jury testimony is quoted in Chapter 5 — was questioned relentlessly about Michael Casey until, as she wrote in an open letter, "I found myself on trial for reporting leads to the proper authorities." Outside the jury room, the group's members documented a campaign of intimidation. Cosentino's car was sideswiped with her son inside. A bomb detonated in a group member's backyard. Life-threatening calls rotated through whoever took a public role; in Bryant's account, the group changed spokespeople "because whoever took a stand would be subjected to retaliation," and its members feared their phones were tapped throughout. [Grade B: her letter on record; the terror campaign per Bryant's documented research; no perpetrator was ever identified.]

---

**Boys Town's own file.** The institutional foreknowledge is documented and predates everything public. In 1986 — two years before the credit union fell — a Boys Town social worker submitted a detailed report naming victims and perpetrators to the institution's chief executive, Father Val Peter. Peter conducted his own investigation, dismissed the accusations as unfounded, and later stated he had no knowledge of the report's existence — an internal contradiction the record preserves. When the accusations reached the Foster Care Review Board in 1988, Carol Stitt's two-year review produced her verdict, on the record: "nothing happened... For me, it was very clear: the case was not investigated and not pursued because of the accused perpetrators."

Two things now sit side by side. The grand jury's hoax-origin theory — that a fired Boys Town employee fabricated the institution's role out of revenge — coexists with a documented fact the theory never addresses: Boys Town's own leadership had received, investigated, and buried a report of abuse within its walls four years before the scandal. And the institution's response to every subsequent inquiry was refusal — "very uncooperative" on Caradori's lead list, "We have no interest in talking to you folks" to the Yorkshire cameras. [Grade A: the record of the 1986 report and Peter's handling; Grade B: Stitt's characterization.]

**Patterson, from the investigator's notes.** Jerry Lowe's field record adds one detail that completes Lisa's biography in the file: in April 1989 he interviewed "Cornelia (Nelly) Patterson — who had taken back her original name after she escaped from custody of the Webbs." The first accuser's escape from the foster home, and her return to her own name, are the documented frame of everything she said afterward. [Grade B: Lowe's record.]

## CHAPTER 4 — The Investigator

### 4.1 Gary Caradori

Gary Alden Caradori was forty years old when the committee hired him — a former Coast Guardsman, former Nebraska State Patrol officer, founder of a Lincoln private-investigation firm called Caracorp, and by reputation a specialist in finding missing children. He was married to Sandi; they had a son, A.J., eight years old. His assistant was a former trooper named Karen Ormiston. [Grade A: contemporary obituaries and reporting.]

What Caradori believed he was walking into can be read in his working documents. He compiled a lead list — the "Caradori list," a dozen pages of names annotated in his own hand — that mapped the allegations' architecture. It included multiple former residents of the Uta Halee girls' home, each flagged "possible victim of sexual exploitation and satanic cult abuse"; a young man named David Matsen, described as an employee who "stayed in the bedroom in the basement of the Franklin Credit Union at nights. Sexually exploited by Larry King. Travelled with Larry King"; the director of the state Department of Social Services, flagged as possibly aware of "funneling children in certain homes for the purpose of exploitation"; a French Cafe employee named Shelia Calder who "arranged parties [for] Larry King with children," then relocated to Washington state; a Douglas County social services director who had formerly worked for King; a limousine driver who "may have information on kids who were transported via limousine"; a former Boys Town resident, Jeff Hubbell, whom the FBI located on the West Coast but whose whereabouts the FBI declined to give Caradori; a photographer named Andrew Baran who worked for King; a church secretary named Barbara Hawthorne who "may know of monies funneled through a church by Alan Baer to currently support Larry King"; a Grace Apostolic Church minister named William Barlow who counseled King-abuse victims and declined to cooperate; a school-placement officer, Naila Aschenbrunner, with possible knowledge of Citron; and a former city employee, Lauri Meyers, who had remitted pornographic photographs to the police; and dozens more. [Grade B: the lead list survives in the research record; the annotations are Caradori's own.]

One entry deserves special attention because it is first-person. Of Robert Fenner, the NCUA's general counsel, Caradori wrote: "probable knowledge of evidence removed from the property of Larry King, however, he has denied this. Also informed me that the FBI told him not to cooperate with my investigation." [Grade B: Caradori's investigative report, March 14, 1990, as reproduced in DeCamp's book.]

By mid-December 1989, Caradori had recorded roughly twenty-one hours of videotaped testimony from three young people — Alisha Owen, Troy Boner, and James "Danny" King (no relation to Lawrence) — whose accounts, taken separately, corroborated one another on names, places, and chartered flights. Owen was a twenty-year-old former foster youth serving time for bad checks; Boner a young man with a drug history; Danny King a teenager. All three described, in substance, being used as sexual commodities by King and associates, flown on private aircraft, and passed among prominent men. [Grade B: the tapes exist and are archived; their content is testimony.]

### 4.2 Chicago

On July 11, 1990, Caradori and his son flew to Chicago — by Caradori's account, to attend the Major League Baseball All-Star Game, and, by his widow's later account, to meet a photographer named Rusty Nelson and obtain photographs implicating King. He called Senator Schmit from the road. Schmit recalled the words for the rest of his life: "We've got them by the short hairs." [Grade B: Schmit's recollection, recorded in interviews and in DeCamp's book.]

At about 2:30 a.m. on July 12, 1990 — Caradori's forty-first birthday — his small aircraft broke apart in flight and crashed into a cornfield in Lee County, Illinois, southwest of Chicago. He and A.J. were killed. The wreckage was scattered over three-quarters of a mile. [Grade A: NTSB record.]

What happened next is where the case permanently splits into two readings.

**The official reading.** The National Transportation Safety Board examined the wreckage and found, in its own words, "no evidence... to suggest any external influence" in the crash. The probable cause involved in-flight structural breakup — the wings overstressing and snapping off — with pilot fatigue, spatial disorientation, and possible instrument malfunction in the dark over farmland. The cause sequence was never fully determined. The FBI, as the lead federal agency in the Franklin matter, impounded Caradori's investigative records within a day of the crash and subpoenaed his widow for all Franklin materials within two days. [Grade A: NTSB; Grade A: the FBI record-impoundment is documented by the committee and DeCamp.]

**The critical reading.** Senator Schmit wrote to the NTSB in December 1990 and again in March 1991. He noted that the plane's rear seats were never recovered. He relayed that he had been told a phosphorus-type bomb "would, in fact, vaporize metal and any other material with which it came in contact and that unless someone knew what they were looking for, it would be difficult, if not impossible, to detect." And he wrote: "I am sure there will be those who will scoff at such a suggestion, but there have been entirely too many violent deaths associated with this investigation." [Grade B: Schmit's letters, on the record.]

Bryant reports additional anomalies from the widow's account: the personal effects returned by the NTSB included Caradori's thirty-five-millimeter camera, undamaged — with the film removed and inexplicably developed — and excluded the leather briefcase his wife had given him as a birthday gift, which his assistant described as so constant a companion it was "virtually an extension" of him. If the photographs existed, the briefcase is where they would have been. [Grade B/C: the widow's and Ormiston's accounts; the briefcase was never recovered.]

Sandie Caradori has said ever since that she believes her husband was murdered. The NTSB found no sabotage. Both statements are in the record. What is also in the record is what the crash did to the investigation: the only trained, independent investigator with subpoena-backed momentum was dead, his files were in FBI custody, and the committee's work effectively died with him. Loran Schmit was told on the legislative floor, in an anonymous phone call he reported publicly, to drop Franklin because it would reach "the highest levels of the Republican Party." [Grade B: Schmit's account, on record; Grade B: the call is also referenced in the Yorkshire Television documentary.]

### 4.3 The crash's echo

Troy Boner, who had been scheduled to meet Caradori the following week, instead met Sandie Caradori at the funeral — and fled. His mother later swore in an affidavit that when her son saw FBI agents at the service, he said they had to leave. He had promised the widow he would recant his recantation. Instead, days later, he appeared before the grand jury and confirmed his repudiation of everything he had told Caradori. [Grade B: Lani Hicks's October 27, 1993 affidavit; Boner's conduct is documented.]

Carol Stitt received a death threat the night before she testified: "If you speak, you won't live to regret it." She gave authorities, by her account, "at least half a square meter of material" on King over the years. "Generally," she said, "the accusations were ignored." [Grade B: her interviews, on record in the documentary and her testimony.]

## CHAPTER 5 — The Grand Juries

### 5.1 Two juries, one conclusion

In early 1990, with Caradori's tapes in hand and his death still weeks away, the machinery of the official response took shape. Attorney General Robert Spire called for a grand jury in January; it was impaneled on March 19, 1990, with Samuel Van Pelt, a former district judge, as special prosecutor and Terry Dougherty as his assistant. Its formal mandate, in its own words, included not only the financial collapse but "a possible adult male prostitution ring operation throughout the country by Franklin principals, and a possible income tax scheme used by an affluent Omahan to finance male homosexual prostitution." [Grade A: the grand jury report's introduction.]

The jury worked eighty-two days, heard seventy-six witnesses, reviewed 395 exhibits and thirty-plus hours of videotape — and King himself was never before it. He was, during the jury's life, a federal pretrial detainee in an out-of-state psychiatric facility, having been committed on the morning of the Bush fundraiser; the jury noted it had asked him to appear voluntarily and he declined, and concluded a subpoena would have produced only a Fifth Amendment plea. [Grade A: the report's own account.]

On July 24, 1990, the jury returned its report, signed by foreman Michael Flanagan. Its famous finding: the abuse allegations were "a carefully crafted hoax, scripted by a person or persons with considerable knowledge of the people and institutions of Omaha." It indicted Alisha Owen on eight counts of perjury and Paul Bonacci on three. [Grade A: the report; indictment records.]

A federal grand jury, impaneled since September 1988 on the financial side and extended to examine the abuse claims in April 1990, returned a three-page report on September 25, 1990: "absolutely no evidence" of interstate transport of children or involvement by any prominent Omahans. It indicted Owen on eight additional federal counts. [Grade A: AP/New York Times, September 25-27, 1990. Her sixteen total counts — eight state, eight federal — explain the varying figures in the record.]

### 5.2 The recantation engine

To understand the hoax verdict, you must understand what it stood on. It stood almost entirely on two recantations.

Troy Boner and Danny King told the grand juries that they had lied to Caradori — that Owen had scripted them, motivated by dreams of book, television, and movie money. Boner swore he had not been paid, threatened, or promised anything to retract. Danny King, on the stand, had first told Caradori the events did not happen, and was, in the grand jury's words, "still persuaded to corroborate Owen's story" anyway. [Grade A: the grand jury report's account of its own witnesses.]

The problem is what the years did to those recantations, and it is documented in court records, sworn affidavits, and the appellate opinion in State v. Owen.

First, the Caracorp phone records. Boner had claimed Owen "scripted" him in a phone call. Caradori's firm's phone records show the call never happened. [Grade B: the records were produced at trial; the point appears in the trial record.]

Second, the FBI polygraph. Boner passed an FBI polygraph — administered by the same agents he later accused of pressuring him — while maintaining, at the time, that his recantation was true. The leak to the Lincoln press that he had *failed* a polygraph was false; it ran anyway. [Grade B: the polygraph report of April 19, 1990 exists in the case record; the leak is documented by researchers citing Lincoln Journal and World-Herald stories.]

Third, Danny King's own words. On the stand at Owen's trial, under questioning by prosecutor Gerald Moran, Danny King was asked whether it was true that he had made the whole story up. His answer: "Yeah, I was — I wasn't really making nothing up about any of the persons." The transcript shows the prosecutor moving on. [Grade A: State v. Owen trial record, as cited in the appellate record and by researchers.]

Fourth, and most completely: on October 27, 1993 — two years after Owen's conviction — Boner swore a fourteen-page affidavit. Its words are the most quoted in the entire case, and they deserve to be quoted in full: "I lied at the Grand Jury hearings, and I lied at the Alisha Owen trial. I lied when I 'recanted' my original statement to Gary Caradori. I lied because I truly believed and still do believe that it was a situation where I must either 'lie or die,' and at the insistence primarily of the Federal Bureau of Investigation officials who were dealing with me at that time, specifically Mr. Mott and Mr. Culver." He named Larry King, Alan Baer, Robert Wadman, Peter Citron, and Eugene Mahoney as men he had been involved with from age fourteen to seventeen, stated that he had delivered Alisha Owen to Police Chief Wadman, and said he believed his brother Shawn's death had been murder designed to ensure his testimony. He demanded side-by-side polygraph examinations with his named men — the tests were never conducted. His mother, Lani Hicks, swore a companion affidavit the same day, confirming that the family had supported his lying "to save our family and himself," that his brother had died on Air Force base housing, and that Troy had told his detailed story to the British television producers. [Grade B: both affidavits, notarized, October 27, 1993; on the record. Boner also admitted in the affidavit that he had, under pressure, delivered Owen to Wadman — an admission that cut both ways, and was treated by no one.]

On October 16, 1990, weeks after Caradori's death, Bonacci had already told the Franklin Committee under oath what had happened to him in Van Pelt's back room: "They kept telling me that if I stuck to my story they were going to make me be in trouble for it... They stuck me in this room. He was sitting in this back room behind this desk... He kind of said, if you stick to the same story that you have been telling, you will be in a lot of trouble." John DeCamp, who was present, identified him: "The guy with the mustache was Sam Van Pelt." Three witnesses — Bonacci, Owen, and a woman identified only as Jane Doe — subsequently charged that Van Pelt had intimidated them. Jane Doe testified that Van Pelt told her how to testify, revealed secret grand jury information to her, alluded to her past in a way she experienced as a threat, and struck her as planning "to use me to discredit Alisha." [Grade B: committee testimony, on the record.]

### 5.3 Inside the grand jury's own report

The complete text of the grand jury report — obtained in full for this document — contains passages that almost never appear in retellings. Presented without commentary, because they speak for themselves:

- On its own evidentiary standard: "Since this investigation included allegations that certain persons were transported by charter flights for illegal purposes, better passenger records would have facilitated the Grand Jury's work." [Grade A.]
- On the male prostitution question it was mandated to examine: "No witnesses called by the Grand Jury had first-hand knowledge... potential key witnesses are no longer in the state of Nebraska, or were successful in evading service of a subpoena. The federal and local authorities should continue their efforts to locate these persons [and] investigate and prosecute violators, if any are found to exist." [Grade A — the hoax verdict coexisting with an open recommendation to keep hunting.]
- On traveling companions of King: "We interviewed a number of witnesses who traveled extensively with King, and a few made what must have been embarrassing admissions about engaging in sexual contact with King on some of these trips." [Grade A.]
- On King himself: "We also found probable cause to believe that King, on numerous occasions, used money or items of value to 'entice, inveigle, persuade, encourage or procure' men in their late teens or early twenties to engage in acts of prostitution with him, and, therefore, he committed the crime of pandering." The jury deferred to the forty pending federal counts; no pandering charge was ever filed by anyone. "Should the matters pending against King in the federal system come to naught, we implore state authorities to act upon the overwhelming evidence against King relating to both pandering and theft offenses." The federal system took a plea on the money; no one ever acted on the rest. [Grade A.]
- On cults: "There is evidence of limited cult activity in eastern Nebraska." [Grade A — the jury's own finding, in its own words.]
- On Omaha: "The Omaha Police Department estimates there are over 500 known pedophiles in the Omaha area." [Grade A.]
- On Robert Andresen, a Union Pacific employee named by witnesses: "a white male, 6'5", 325 lbs., has been known to coerce males (including male minors) to his residence. Once he has taken a male there, he may sexually assault and/or pornographically photograph the young man. Furthermore, we have reviewed documents that state Andresen has threatened to physically harm his victim(s) and has threatened to search out and kill those who report him to the authorities. Unfortunately, because the victims refuse to testify against him, we could not indict." [Grade A — a named, described, documented predator, unindicted, on the jury's own page.]
- On Alice King: she took the Fifth; "Mrs. King was intimidated by King mentally and physically." [Grade A.]
- On the "reasonable behavior" passage, in full context: "As previously stated, children do have the right to expect that if they exhibit reasonable behavior, they will not be abused... In too many cases it is the adult rather than the child who is protected." [Grade A — the soundbite is real; the surrounding paragraph criticizes adult protection, not children.]
- On the evidence it saw: the jury faulted Caradori's videotapes as "stopped and started at suspicious intervals," and concluded Caradori had been "duped into working with Casey." [Grade A — the jury's characterization; the counter-evidence — Ormiston's and others' polygraphs clearing Caradori of coaching — is Grade B.]

The report also records the hoax origin theory: Michael Casey, who had been fired from Boys Town in 1974 for stealing confidential records, nursed a grievance and built the story from street rumors, gay-community contacts, and resentments. Its crucial admission: "We believe that Caradori would not have found Owen if it were not for Casey. Owen then led Caradori to other potential victim/witnesses." Every witness downstream of Owen — the entire corroboration structure — traces, by the grand jury's own account, through the figure it deemed a con man. [Grade A.]

### 5.4 The committee's answer

The Franklin Committee's formal response to the hoax verdict survives in full, and it is the standing counter-document of the official record: "We are puzzled by the choice of words and the basis for the conclusion... As Committee members who viewed the tapes, interrogated our investigator, and looked at other corroborating circumstances, we wonder how the grand jury was able to differentiate between 'a carefully crafted hoax' and the truth. To the extent that the conclusion was based on the now famous recantation by Troy Boner, perhaps it was the grand jury that was the victim of the hoax. Mr. Boner has now betrayed himself as a pathological liar. If the grand jury believed Mr. Boner over Alisha Owen, who stands by her story, the hoax may well have been perpetrated on the grand jury." And: "If it was 'carefully crafted,' who crafted it and when?" And, on the asymmetry of the perjury charges: "Alisha Owen and Paul Bonacci are charged with perjury and Troy Boner and Danny King are not... the victims who stand by their story are charged with perjury, while those that have admitted to false statements before the Committee are not. That makes little sense to us. Either all of them should have been indicted or none of them. The message is mixed and appears to favor encouraging the recanting as a way to avoid the hazards of criminal prosecution. It also tells persons they can lie under oath to Legislative Committees, so long as they change their story before they get to court." And the grand jury's own explanation for why Harold Andersen's name had appeared in the testimony deserves the record's exact words: his inclusion was "probably politically generated" — an attribution of motive to the accusers, offered without examination of the fact that Andersen chaired the advisory board of the institution at the center of the case. [Grade A: committee report text; Grade A: the grand jury report.]

### 5.5 The jury's witnesses, in its own words

The report's individual findings deserve the same verbatim treatment as its famous conclusion, because they are the substrate of the verdict:

- On Bonacci: "perhaps the most pathetic witness... a child victim of repeated sexual abuse by persons not associated with the Franklin investigation. He now stands convicted and sentenced on three separate charges of sexual abuse of a minor. He has told numerous stories, recanted the same, and then told more on videotape to Caradori. Even while testifying before us, he admitted that some of his Grand Jury testimony was untrue. He has been diagnosed as having multiple personalities, and his psychiatrist doubts that he can tell the truth." The jury indicted him — and its report simultaneously certified him as a multiply-diagnosed abuse victim whose stories kept changing. [Grade A.]

- On Boner: the tapes were made "solely for the purpose of corroboration of allegations made by Owen"; his motive was "monetary gain via litigation plus movie/book rights"; he recanted; he "was not paid, threatened, nor promised anything to retract." [Grade A — the jury's finding; the affidavit and phone records that undermine it are Grade B and in Chapter 5.2.]

- On Danny King: "After repeatedly telling Caradori that the events did not take place, Danny King was still persuaded to corroborate Owen's story" — with the same claimed motive. [Grade A.]

- On Owen: "might have been sexually abused during her early years, but not by the people and in the way she has alleged." [Grade A — both grand juries conceded abuse while denying the named perpetrators.]

- On the recantations' legal status, the report is silent on a point DeCamp pressed for years: by retracting to the grand jury, Boner and Danny King were admitting they had lied to the Legislature — a separately chargeable perjury — and the grand jury "made no mention of the fact." The men who recanted walked; the woman who did not went to prison. [Grade A/B.]

- On the news media, the jury's Section VII criticized the committee's leak of the tapes, noted that three-fourths of hotline callers responded anonymously, faulted the presentation of polygraph evidence, recorded that KKAR's station manager Steve Brown and Michael Casey "lived under the same roof" in 1989 and "freely exchanged information" — "render suspect KKAR's enthusiastic coverage of Franklin" — and then editorialized: "We believe that it is unfortunate that the Omaha area has only one major newspaper. Credible competition would give the public the satisfaction of an alternative viewpoint. The untrue and unwarranted personal attack on Harold Andersen can, in part, be attributed to public resentment and competing media jealousy which naturally flow from the Omaha World-Herald's dominant position." A grand jury, having just cleared the monopoly publisher by name, pronounced the publisher's misfortune. [Grade A.]

### 5.6 The affidavit no one answered

On May 28, 1994, four years after the verdict and four years before the FirsTier settlements would surface, Loran Schmit swore an affidavit. It records that on the morning of July 11, 1990 — the morning Caradori died — two attorneys representing Alan Baer, one named Selle, met with Schmit, committee counsel Robert Creager, and a staff attorney in Schmit's Capitol office regarding the Franklin investigation. And it records what those attorneys said, with everybody present, sworn to by a man who had been a state senator for twenty-two years:

"These two attorneys advised us with everybody present that the F.B.I. officials dealing with them and with Alan Baer had instructed Mr. Alan Baer to 'keep your (his) head down, or you'll (he) will get it blown off, your (his) mouth shut and to talk to no one.'"

The meeting occurred on the crash morning itself; the affiant was the committee's chairman; the subject was the committee's investigation; the threat attributed to the FBI was never prosecuted, never investigated by anyone, and never publicly answered by the Bureau. It rests on the word of one honorable man — but it is sworn, dated, specific, and uncontradicted, and it describes the federal government's lead agency instructing a named witness in a child-abuse investigation to say nothing, on pain of death, in language a Nebraskan farmer would understand. It belongs in any honest account of why some people believed, and believe, that the case was not merely mishandled. [Grade B: sworn, uncontradicted, single-source.]

**The cleared-by-name practice.** Against normal grand jury practice — indict, or say nothing — the jury departed from its mandate in one direction only. It cleared by name: Judge Theodore Carlson, Harold Andersen, Eugene Mahoney, Mayor P.J. Morgan, attorney Thomas McKenney. It wrote, of Wadman: "We now look upon Owen as the perpetrator and Wadman as the victim." And it ruled a person called "Larry the Kid" — described independently by four children over three years, as "King Horse," as "Larry (Lnuk)," as "Larry Jr.," and as "Larry Little King" — to be fictitious, without explaining how four children invented the same personality across years. [Grade A: the report; the four-children point is DeCamp's documented observation.]

**Van Pelt's road to the jury.** The special prosecutor who received the jury's thanks had been a district judge who resigned the bench — "being a judge an unpleasant job" — served as Nebraska's insurance director, and in 1984 was appointed by Governor Bob Kerrey to investigate the state police shooting of Arthur Kirk, a case in which a man was shot nine times and the death ruled a suicide on the statements of the shooters themselves, with no ballistics tests. Forty-three of Kirk's neighbors called Van Pelt "a hired gun for the state" in a signed letter; Kirk's widow said she had been "used as a decoy"; the World-Herald defended him editorially against "wild, sensational rumors." He later wrote a book of judicial recollections with an unprintable title and co-authored a biography of Senator Exon. [Grade A: obituary and records; Grade B: the Kirk letter.]

**The hotline.** The jury's report records that over three-fourths of the hotline callers responded anonymously — a statistical monument to the fear the case generated in the population it touched. [Grade A.]

---

**The report's architecture.** For completeness, the document's own map of itself: Section I addresses the credit union and the Kings; Section II, "The Rumormongers," profiles Michael Casey, Kirsten Hallberg, and Bonnie Cosentino; Section III, "The Suicides," examines Charlie Rogers and Curtis Tucker; Section IV, "The Early Allegations," dissects the Webb case and the Smith case and names every official failure; Section V, of which the public text survives in part, addresses the committee's own members — and of Senator Chambers it says, in the recovered text: "Chambers spoke grandly about justice being done while at the same time undermining the democratic judicial process in its most basic form... It is difficult to understand how King and his entourage led an extravagant and outlandish lifestyle for so many years in Chambers' own district, without his having any inkling." Section VI, "The Politicians," per the surviving secondary record, extended that treatment to the committee's chairman. Section VII, "The News Media," is quoted in Chapter 5.5. Section VIII carries the recommendations — a state grand jury system, better charter-flight records, cult-awareness training for police, independent monitoring of the child-protective system, a state district-attorney system to insulate prosecutors from "community pressure," stronger pedophile-reporting, and NCUA audit reform. Exhibit A is the interim Webb report quoted in Chapter 3; Exhibit B carries the recommendation sets. The document, in other words, is not the single paragraph history remembers. [Grade A: the full text as captured.]

---

**Casey, in the report's own portrait.** Michael Casey: fired from Boys Town in August 1974 for stealing confidential records in pursuit of a book and movie; a resume with a fraudulent degree; a criminal record dating to 1960; hospitalized at St. Joseph's in November 1988, where he met Owen in the special-care unit and gave her name to Caradori. The grand jury called him "a 'con man' passing himself off as an investigative reporter" who "endeavored to uncover the 'real' Franklin story" from street rumors and grievance — and its Boys Town theory held that the institution was "written into the Franklin scripts" because Casey had vowed to "get even" for his 1974 firing. One admission, already quoted, does the most work in the critical reading: by the jury's own account, every witness Caradori taped reached him through Casey and Owen. The man the jury made the hoax's author is also the man the jury credits with creating the entire evidentiary record. [Grade A: the report.]

---

**The jury's own voice.** The report's acknowledgments thank "Special Prosecutor, Judge Samuel Van Pelt, and Assistant Special Prosecutor, Terry Dougherty, for their professional leadership, management, and organization" — and nowhere in its eighty-two days of work does the document note that those it cleared by name had sat in the rooms where its foreman earned his living, or that its star witnesses' recantations were themselves admissions of legislative perjury. Citizen juries swear an oath to return indictments "diligently and fairly"; the historical record cannot tell us whether this one did, and this document does not pretend otherwise. It can tell us exactly what the jury wrote, who helped write it, and what was never said in it. [Grade A; the final sentence is the file's analytical position.]

## CHAPTER 6 — The Trials

### 6.1 Alisha Owen's trial

Alisha Owen was tried in the spring of 1991 on eight state counts of perjury for her grand jury testimony. The trial consumed five weeks, 4,000 pages of testimony, 182 exhibits, and roughly eleven hours of videotape. She was defended, pro bono, by Henry Rosenthal, a veteran Omaha attorney who treated it as the case of his life. Prosecutors Gerald Moran and Robert Sigler carried the state's case before District Judge Raymond J. Case. [Grade A: State v. Owen, 510 N.W.2d 503 (Neb. Ct. App. 1993).]

The jury convicted her on June 21, 1991; within minutes the state dropped the perjury charges against Paul Bonacci. The next morning the World-Herald editorialized: "Prosecution Crushed Lies With Avalanche of Truth." She was sentenced on August 8, 1991, to three-to-five years on each count, stacked to a nine-to-fifteen-year range (the DeCamp book's "nine to twenty-seven" reflects the maximum consecutive stack; both figures derive from the same sentence). [Grade A: appellate opinion; USA Today; Omaha World-Herald.]

The appellate record preserved by the Nebraska Court of Appeals reads, for critics, like a catalog. Several jurors watched CBS's "48 Hours" Franklin segment during deliberations after defying the judge's instruction — and Judge Case had told the jury the day before that the program would air, announcing the broadcast time. A letter from Michael Casey — never admitted into evidence — was read aloud in the jury room; juror affidavits described written statements shown to the panel that were never presented in court and that "mysteriously could not be located after the trial." Jurors brought dictionary definitions of "reasonable" and "doubt" into the room. Rosenthal told the court the videotapes shown to the grand jury had been altered — the mutual corroboration between Owen and Boner edited out — citing the trial transcript at page 2,842; Moran objected on recantation-grounds precedent, and Judge Case sided with Moran, then insinuated against Sandie Caradori ("What's her part in this?") when he learned the original tapes came from the widow, and Moran accused Rosenthal's office of erasing them. Rosenthal's post-trial affidavits included one from private investigator Robert Janecek, a former law-enforcement officer who swore that three of Boner's relatives had told him Boner accepted a $12,000 payment for lying at the trial — a bribe paired with the warning that he would "never make it to trial" otherwise. [Grade A: the appellate opinion confirms the 48 Hours viewing, the Casey letter, and the Janecek and Donna Owen affidavits; Grade B: the altered-tapes claim and the $12,000 figure rest on Rosenthal's representation and Janecek's sworn statement.]

The blood-test question — whether Police Chief Robert Wadman had fathered Owen's child, as she had claimed in interviews years earlier — collapsed on multiple fronts: genetic testing by three laboratories excluded Wadman with total certainty (the grand jury report noted this); Medicaid records showed Owen had named a different man as the father on applications between 1985 and 1988; her own first lawyer's failure to file a timely appeal on her bad-check conviction became the seed of an ineffective-assistance claim; and that lawyer, Pamela Vuchetich, was by then romantically involved with FBI agent Mickey Mott — the FBI's lead agent in the case and a key witness against Owen. Post-conviction counsel James Fogarty called it "the most devastating case of attorney-client betrayal" he had encountered; the FBI appeared at her state bar proceeding and the charges were dropped, the files sealed. Vuchetich — licensed in Nebraska for forty-three years as of this writing, still listed as practicing in Lincoln — withdrew from Owen's defense shortly before trial, saying Owen intended to commit perjury; a year earlier she had told the World-Herald she still believed Owen's account. [Grade A: appellate opinion and bar records; Grade B: Fogarty's characterization; the Mott relationship appears in Owen's letters and the post-conviction motion.]

Owen's appeal produced a split outcome: the conviction stood, but the appeals court vacated the denial of her new-trial motion and ordered an evidentiary hearing before a different judge on the jury-misconduct claims; a second round of appeals eventually ended with the convictions affirmed. She served roughly four and a half years, including about two years in solitary confinement — the Parole Board nonetheless voted unanimously, five to zero, to parole her at her first eligibility in September 2000, a member calling it the most impressive inmate record the board had seen. Senator Chambers wrote in support, citing among her prison accomplishments her mentoring of other inmates toward their GEDs and her work with nontraditional students at the College of St. Mary's. In November 1999, the Board of Pardons — Governor Mike Johanns, Secretary of State Scott Moore, Attorney General Don Stenberg — had voted three to zero to deny her even a hearing on commutation. Her parole was revoked in 1997, one week after Inside Edition aired a segment suggesting paperboy Johnny Gosch was still alive, and restored after further proceedings; she was finally free by 2000. [Grade A: Omaha World-Herald, November 3, 1999; AP, February 9, 2000; appellate records; Bryant's reporting.]

She has never recanted. Today she is Alisha Bystrom — married twenty-nine years, a travel-business owner in Omaha, the mother of a daughter born when she was fifteen and raised by her parents during her incarceration. She serves as Associate Director and Events Coordinator of Epstein Justice, a nonprofit founded by Nick Bryant, and her stated goal is a Truth and Reconciliation Commission. [Grade A/B: the organization's own materials; interviews including a detailed 2026 podcast appearance.]

### 6.2 Paul Bonacci

Paul Bonacci's legal record contains more turns than anyone else's. Convicted on three counts of sexual abuse of a minor in a matter separate from Franklin, he was indicted by the grand jury for perjury — charges dropped the day Owen was convicted. He sued the Omaha Public Schools for failing to act on his reports to school counselors; the Eighth Circuit dismissed on the DeShaney principle that the state has no constitutional duty to protect against private violence. He sued King, Citron, Baer, Andersen, Wadman, and others in 1991; everyone was dismissed on procedural grounds or by summary judgment except King, who never responded. In 1999, Senior Judge Warren Urbom entered a default judgment against King for $800,000 in compensatory and $200,000 in punitive damages, accepting as pleaded that from December 1980 to 1988 King had repeatedly assaulted Bonacci, organized rituals, and forced him to "scavenge" for other children. Urbom, who had called Bonacci's testimony "bizarre," noted that King's failure to respond made the allegations "true as to him" — a procedural default, never tested at trial, never paid. King dropped his appeal of the judgment in January 2000. [Grade A: court records; Grade A for the procedural nature of the default.]

Diagnosed with multiple personality disorder by three state-appointed psychiatrists, Bonacci named, in a 1993 interview with former FBI agent Ted Gunderson, an abuser chronology beginning in 1976; researchers have confirmed that several of the names he attached to the network — obscure members of the North American Man/Boy Love Association — were real, documented members, an unusual level of specificity for a supposed fabulist. His claims extend to the Johnny Gosch kidnapping and to a military-intelligence programming framework; the FBI has formally stated it does not consider him a reliable witness and has never relied on him. He is alive, married, self-employed in construction management, maintains his account — and his 1999 deposition was released as a film in July 2024, the only visual record of a Franklin witness under oath. [Grade B: the diagnosis and the NAMBLA corroboration; Grade C: the Gosch and programming claims.]

### 6.3 The Webbs, and the dismissal that closed the file

Jarrett and Barbara Webb were never tried. The grand jury recommended charges; the county attorney did not file them for months; and on December 26, 1990, a Washington County judge threw the case out on statute-of-limitations grounds, dating the assaults to two days after Lisa's sixteenth birthday and declining to apply Nebraska's tolling rule for minors. Lisa — the witness whose polygraph the grand jury itself credited — was never cross-examined by any jury. She is alive; an Omaha newspaper located her in 2018, raising a son who had been through the juvenile justice system she had once warned the state about. [Grade A: court record; Grade B: The Reader, Omaha, March 2018.]

### 6.4 The counts, and the letter in the jury room

The eight perjury counts, in substance: the Twin Towers parties of August 1983, where Owen said she was taken by a Boys Town resident, Richard "Jeff" Hubbell — who testified he left a dance before eleven and never saw her again, and disclaimed knowing any of the named men; Harold Andersen fondling a twelve-year-old boy at the parties; Police Chief Wadman molesting her before her fifteenth birthday and in twenty to twenty-five motel encounters across a year, including an encounter in Council Bluffs, and fathering her child; and Judge Theodore Carlson at the French Cafe reception. Each count failed on witness testimony or records; the French Cafe's general manager and the judge's own Saturday-by-Saturday alibi did the most visible work. [Grade A: trial record.]

The letter read in the jury room was dated March 15, 1990 — Michael Casey to Owen, in prison. Its key passage: "Promises were made — before I ever gave Gary your name and location — he'd assured me that if your info checked out you would receive favorable consideration and protection outside of a prison environment. I feel most responsible if promises were made to you and not kept!" A greeting card followed on March 23: three national publications and a movie producer were interested, and Owen was "assured of a job... as a consultant and researcher." The letter was never received into evidence. It was read aloud to the jury. And it "mysteriously could not be located after the trial"; Owen argued it had been planted. Bill Gehrig testified that Owen had told him in January 1990 she was working with Casey on a Franklin report — testimony the defense could not shake. The grand jury had already called Casey "a 'con man' passing himself off as an investigative reporter" — a man with a criminal record since 1960, fired from Boys Town in 1974 for stealing confidential records, whose grievance the grand jury made the engine of the hoax. The defense made one objection in sixteen pages of cross-examination bearing on the World-Herald's editorial war against Schmit and DeCamp's role. [Grade A: appellate record; Grade B: the planting claim.]

One litigation footnote that belongs to this chapter because it predates everything: in 1984-1987, a woman named Crystal Chambers sued the Omaha Girls Club — King's old organization — together with the World-Herald, Harold Andersen, Woodson Howe, John Gottschalk, the attorney general, and the governor, alleging that the interlocking boards (the publisher's wife sat on the Club's board; Club officers sat on the state equal-opportunity commission) had prejudiced her employment case and driven the paper's coverage. Judge Urbom recused; Chief Judge Beam granted summary judgment to the paper's men — no inference of agreement could be drawn from the interlocks alone. The institutional configuration that would define the Franklin era — publisher, his paper, his wife's board seats, and the Girls Club — had already been a federal litigation subject before the first allegation was ever made. [Grade A: Chambers v. Omaha Girls Club, 629 F. Supp. 925 (D. Neb. 1986), aff'd (8th Cir. 1987).]

---

**The procedure, in detail.** The state struck five of the six women on the jury panel and one alternate; Owen's challenge under the then-recent Batson doctrine failed on the law of the era, which had not yet extended the anti-discrimination rule to gender. Judge Case admitted the blood-test results over no defense objection — waiving the chain-of-custody fight that later writers said should have been made — and admitted Owen's presentence report over a successful privilege objection that the appellate court nonetheless deemed harmless. Case refused to recuse himself from the new-trial motion; the appeals court held that refusal an abuse of discretion depriving Owen of a substantial right and ordered the hearing before a different judge. Juror affidavits described being told to accept hearsay, and being shown the written statements that were never admitted and never found again. On the Miranda question — Owen's prison interrogations — Case ruled there had been no custodial interrogation: investigators had approached her as a possible victim, counsel was present for the formal sessions, and she was, in the ruling's phrase, free to leave the room. The "48 Hours" segment the jurors watched featured Boner recanting, the former police chief on devastation, and a defense attorney on the volatility of child-abuse allegations; the trial judge had announced its air time to the jury himself, the evening before deliberations began. [Grade A: appellate record; Grade B: juror affidavits.]

---

**Owen, before the tapes.** Her background, from the trial record: an honor student and college-newspaper editor; a party at fifteen where older boys — "a few of them were associated with Boys Town," she has said — invited her onward; a spiral of parties and drugs; bad-check charges at nineteen; the suicide attempt and the St. Joseph's psychiatric stay in late 1988, where she met Michael Casey, who gave her name to Caradori; the baby at fifteen, raised by her parents, whose paternity she attributed to Wadman — excluded by three laboratories — and whom she named Amanda Jayne. Her parents stood by her from the day she told them; her father, she says, taught her the family rule that the sin is the standing-by. Her brother Aaron was seventeen, in custody on a joyriding charge, when he died the week of her lawyer's disciplinary hearing. [Grade A/B: trial record, her accounts, Bryant's reporting.]

**The quashed subpoenas.** Post-conviction counsel James Fogarty subpoenaed Vuchetich, Mott, the U.S. Attorney's office prosecutor, and Culver. The United States moved to quash; Judge Livingston granted the motion, ruling that the disclosures "would reveal investigative techniques and procedures." Fogarty, on the record: the FBI must be "a dream job for every healthy, heterosexual young man" — a joke with a blade in it, since the agent at the center had just been named in his client's letters. [Grade A: the ruling; Grade B: the quip.]

---

**The federal grand jury, from inside.** Owen appeared before the extended federal grand jury on June 21, 1990 — three weeks before Caradori's death. Her account, given to the committee under oath: the prosecutor Thalken "attempted to terrorize" her into recanting; a prosecutor was, in DeCamp's retelling of her words, "livid and discharging spittle" at her refusal. She had signed a voluntary agreement in January 1990 to take a lie-detector test regarding the Wadman paternity question; the FBI declined to administer it; an independent examiner's test, taken instead, supported her account. The federal jury indicted her eight times that September. Of the entire federal experience, the committee's counsel said the questioning was "always accusatory... they just never asked her any questions at all" beyond the demand to recant. [Grade B: her sworn testimony; the polygraph agreement in the record.]

---

**The counts, and what defeated each:**

| Count | Allegation | What the jury heard |
|---|---|---|
| I | Twin Towers party, Aug 1983 | Hubbell's testimony; the building's own records |
| II | Second Twin Towers party | Same |
| III | Andersen fondling a boy | Andersen's denial; his thank-you-party account |
| IV | Wadman, pre-15th birthday | Wadman's denial; three-lab exclusion |
| V | Wadman, 20-25 motel encounters | No corroboration; blood test |
| VI-VII | Continuation counts | Records contradicted dates |
| VIII | Judge Carlson, French Cafe | GM Moore: no such party; Carlson's Saturday alibi |

The trial record, read whole, is a catalogue of a case built on testimony with no corroborating documents — which is both the prosecution's strongest point (the documents that would corroborate were never required to exist, as the grand jury itself admitted) and its weakest (the witnesses' credibility was the case, and the defense attacked exactly there). [Grade A: trial record; the analysis is the file's.]

---

**The prison record.** At York, Owen spent approximately two years in solitary confinement — attacked in the shower by three inmates weeks after state investigators visited her unit and, in the resulting isolation, interviewed repeatedly by the FBI toward a recantation she never made. A supporter's research claimed the duration was the longest imposed on a female citizen in the state's history; the claim is Grade C and unverified, and the documented months are grave enough without it. Her parole was revoked in September 1997, one week after Inside Edition aired a segment suggesting Johnny Gosch was alive — a revocation without stated explanation — and restored on appeal; she was free by 2000. [Grade A: the parole timeline; Grade B: the shower attack per Bryant; Grade C: the superlative.]

---

**The workbook.** Among the archived materials is a document unique in the case: Owen's account in her own hand, annotated by Caradori as he worked it, each entry carrying his own "Corroboration:" or "Discrepancy:" tasking. Its contents, as recorded there: the March 1984 first private flight — a small Cessna from Sky Harbor, fueled in Denver, roughly seven hours to Los Angeles, its manifest reading Larry King, a man from the French Cafe, one pilot, two young boys listed only as "Aaron" and "Jeremy," Troy Boner, and Danny King; the drug runs for Alan Baer, a commercial flight under an alias with cocaine worn around the waist, Baer waiting at the Omaha airport — which Boner told differently, the annotation noting the conflict and tasking the records; the bad-check scheme, in which Baer allegedly gave Boner $1,450 in front of ConAgra and covered checks across several states while the pair were prosecuted for only a few — because, in the account, King and Baer wanted them out of town as too old and too risky. There were the Quality Inn meetings in Bellevue, monthly, in a room registered in Boner's name as late as 1988. There was the photographer Nelson, developing his own film, keeping the negatives — "possibly to blackmail Larry King at a later date" — with photos of Andersen and Baer in an album at the Twin Towers. And there was the black dress Wadman allegedly bought her, preserved as an exhibit, alongside photographs of her with the child she believed his. The workbook also preserves its own audit trail. A flight date given as October 1977 — impossible, at age eight or nine, under her own chronology — is flagged in this file as a transcription error. And Jeff Hubbell, listed among the "disappeared" of 1983-89, carries an annotation recording the FBI's location of him on the West Coast. It is the only document in the case where an investigator graded his own evidence as he gathered it — and its existence means the corroboration structure was neither blind faith nor scripting, but a working matrix the crash froze mid-task. [Grade B: the workbook and its annotations; the date-flag is the file's own audit.]

## CHAPTER 7 — The Officials

Every major figure in the official response has a documented trajectory. Presented without adjectives, because the trajectories speak.

**Robert Wadman, Omaha's police chief.** Utah-raised, the first chief hired from outside the department, in office from 1982 to 1989. On camera in April 1989, he confirmed that King had called him about releasing a suitcase seized in a drug raid — one of "hundreds of those requests" — while King, on the same program, said of the chief, "I felt that I could call anyone in this city." Wadman testified under oath that he barely knew King; had met him regarding community programs and seen him socially perhaps three times. He was fired in 1989 for refusing to sign disciplinary notices in an unrelated matter — an episode in which, the court record shows, he refused to sign documents asserting a political-interference plot he believed did not exist; the Nebraska Supreme Court upheld the firing over a dissent. Inside the department, officers later testified, the King investigation was treated as so sensitive that it was kept from the chief because officers feared he was protecting King; Lieutenant Bill Goodrich told officers that a federal agency investigating King had asked OPD to slow down or back off its own child-pornography probe. A psychologist's evaluation of the investigating officer, requested by Wadman's office, followed. Wadman today is Professor Emeritus of Criminal Justice at Weber State University in Utah, listed as emeritus through the 2026-27 academic year. [Grade A: Wadman v. City of Omaha; the KETV interviews; Grade B: the internal-suppression accounts via Dennis Carlson's testimony and DeCamp's record.]

**Thomas Thalken, the federal prosecutor.** The U.S. Attorney whose office ran the federal grand jury told state officials, through the attorney general's office, that the federal investigation was "confined to the money issues" and not investigating child abuse. He became a U.S. Magistrate Judge and retired January 1, 2017. His son chairs the Omaha Personnel Board and serves as a special assistant attorney general; his niece is a county clerk-magistrate in Wahoo. [Grade A: testimony records; judicial records; namesakes registered.]

**Michael Heavican, the county attorney who became Chief Justice.** As Lancaster County Attorney in the late 1980s, Heavican filed a civil petition alleging that John DeCamp and his wife had abused their daughter — DeCamp calls it retaliation; Heavican, through a spokesman at the time, denied it was related to Franklin. Heavican went on to serve as U.S. Attorney, and then as Chief Justice of the Nebraska Supreme Court from 2006 until his retirement on October 31, 2024 — eighteen years as the head of the state's judiciary. He never, in any public record located, addressed the Franklin chapter of his career. [Grade A: biographical record; Grade B: DeCamp's characterization.]

**Samuel Van Pelt, the special prosecutor.** A former district judge who had resigned the bench, appointed to run the grand jury after Governor Kerrey had earlier tapped him to investigate a controversial state police shooting that ruled a nine-times-shot man a suicide. Van Pelt's grand jury thanked him for his "professional leadership." He later served a term as Lancaster County Sheriff, wrote a book of judicial recollections with a title unprintable here, co-authored a biography of Senator Jim Exon, and spent his final decades as an arbitrator. He died before this document was written; his obituary mentioned the Franklin investigation but not its verdict. [Grade A: obituary and records; Grade B: the Kirk-letter criticism quoted in Chapter 5.]

**Michael Flanagan, the foreman.** A twenty-seven-year Union Pacific legal-department employee — the records custodian — presided over a grand jury whose report cleared prominent men while, by DeCamp's verified investigation, an anonymous Union Pacific executive reported that Flanagan himself had been accused of improper advances toward a young male trainee and that the railroad had paid a substantial settlement to quiet it. The Bonacci civil complaint framed the consequence precisely: the foreman was "being literally asked to indict other individuals... for the very things that he, Flanagan, had been accused of." The settlement was never independently documented. [Grade B/C: DeCamp first-person verification of the underlying report; the settlement claim was never independently confirmed.]

**The judges.** Raymond J. Case, Owen's trial judge — his conduct catalog (the 48 Hours announcement, the subpoena quashes, the Sandi Caradori insinuation) is in the appellate record; his post-bench life is undocumented. Richard Kopf — the magistrate who committed King on the fundraiser morning, sealed the raid warrants, and signed the cell-search warrant — rose to district judge, chief judge, and died in January 2025. William Cambridge, who sentenced King, died in 2004. James Murphy, who ordered the blood test and tried to block DeCamp from representing Bonacci, died in 2017. Everett Inbody, who handled Owen's second appeal, retired in 2018 and is alive at eighty. Warren Urbom, who entered the default judgment against King, died in 2014. Merritt Warren, who sentenced Peter Citron, died in 2020 at ninety-seven, honored by the legislature. David Quist, who dismissed the Webb charges, was confirmed as a sitting judge as early as 1982; his later record is undocumented. [Grade A: judicial records, obituaries.]

**The FBI men.** Nicholas O'Hara, the Omaha special agent-in-charge, declared the allegations lacking "substance" in February 1989 — before agents had interviewed King, and roughly a year before agents interviewed Owen. O'Hara kept Wadman's photograph on his desk and told Schmit and DeCamp in a private meeting, in DeCamp's recollection: "You f--- with Bob Wadman, you f--- with the FBI." Mickey Mott and Culver were the agents who handled Boner and Danny King; Boner's affidavit names them as the men who made him choose between lying and dying; Mott's romantic involvement with Owen's first attorney is documented in the post-conviction record. [Grade B: meeting accounts; affidavits; the relationship appears in sworn filings. The FBI's public file on the Franklin matter, requested under FOIA in 2017, produced the response that no responsive documents exist — a negative finding that speaks to the state of the record rather than to innocence or guilt.]

**The grand jurors.** Citizens, mostly; the foreman a railroad lawyer; no Franklin-era figure among them has ever spoken publicly about the deliberations. [Grade A.]

**The attorney general's office.** Robert Spire received the July 1988 report and passed it along. Don Stenberg, his elected successor, received Ted Gunderson's twelve-point letter alleging obstruction — everything from the FBI's file review of committee materials without permission to the NCUA counsel being told not to cooperate to the entrapment attempt against Owen. His formal reply: his people had been "instructed to watch for any indication of cults or clandestine groups or organizations that traffic in children for sexual purposes." The letter's roster included the assistant attorney general who had received the 1988 report and the deputy who later argued Owen's appeal. [Grade A: the letter and reply, on the record.]

**The FBI layer, in detail.** The Bureau's Omaha office had been examining Franklin since 1987 — more than a year before the raid — a fact the World-Herald itself reported in 1990. Special Agent-in-Charge Nicholas O'Hara was a career officer with a photograph of Robert Wadman on his desk; the agents of record in the abuse file were Mickey Mott and a partner named Culver. Their documented actions, in sequence. February 1989: the public "no substance" declaration, a year before interviewing King or Owen. That same spring: the private meeting with Schmit and DeCamp at which O'Hara issued the warning quoted above. March 9, 1990: the Bureau arranged and taped a phone call from Boner to Owen in her cell — its own transcript showed Owen had not lied — and that same day, under a warrant signed by Magistrate Kopf, searched her cell, producing eight letters postmarked after the search. Spring 1990: Boner passed the FBI polygraph while maintaining the recantation, and a press leak, published, reported that he had failed. February 1990: the instruction to NCUA counsel Robert Fenner not to cooperate with Caradori. Ongoing: the location of Jeff Hubbell on the West Coast, his whereabouts withheld from the committee's investigator. Summer 1990: a review of the legislature's committee files without the chairman's permission, after which items were reported missing. December 1989: the committee's tapes passed into Bureau custody and reached television — by the account a reporter gave Owen — via purchase from FBI agents. And within forty-eight hours of the crash: the impoundment of everything Caradori had. The Bureau's Freedom of Information response to a 2017 request for its Franklin investigation file: no responsive documents. [Grade A for the documented actions; Grade B for the meeting and leak accounts; Grade A for the FOIA negative.]

**The internal OPD war.** Inside the Omaha Police Department, the King investigation was run so quietly that officers handwrote their reports rather than use the steno pool, and when Chief Wadman came to the unit and asked directly whether they were investigating King, the officers lied and said no — because, as one testified to the Foster Care Review Board, they feared he was protecting King. Lieutenant Bill Goodrich's mid-1988 instruction to "slow down or back off" at a federal agency's request was memorialized by Officer Irl Carmean in a December 20, 1988 memo to the deputy chief. Officers Carmean and Hoch pursued leads — Hoch's May 1988 report on a possible King-Nelson child-pornography investigation was discontinued, in Bryant's researched account, "inexplicably," despite documented leads — and both were pulled off the case; Wadman's office then requested a psychological evaluation of Carmean, citing his actions "during the Larry King investigation," in a memo to Public Safety Director Pitmon Foxall. DeCamp records, without independent confirmation, that Foxall was King's cousin; the official OPS history confirms Foxall as one of the force's pioneering Black officers. Officer Chris Carmean (no relation) would testify to the Franklin Committee in June 1989 that Loretta Smith's charges were credible and deserved follow-up — after he had been removed from the case. [Grade A for the memo, the trial testimony, and Wadman's own memo; Grade B for the officers' account of hiding the probe from the chief; Grade C for the Foxall-cousin claim.]

**The foreman's shadow.** The anonymous Union Pacific executive's call to DeCamp — "I am too old to start over... I do not feel I can sit idly by" — alleged that Flanagan had made improper advances toward a young male trainee named Pike and that the railroad had paid a substantial settlement. DeCamp: "I began to investigate, and found the facts accurate." The Bonacci civil complaint put the legal consequence in one sentence: the foreman was "being literally asked to indict other individuals whom Bonacci was identifying for the very things that he, Flanagan, had been accused of and reached civil settlement to avoid like indictment and prosecution." Flanagan was also accused in the same suit of sharing grand jury information with Union Pacific lawyers nearly daily. The settlement was never independently documented; the accusation was never litigated to a conclusion; both rest on Grade B/C material and are recorded as such. What is Grade A: the foreman's employer stood in Franklin's correspondent-bank world; another Union Pacific official sat on Franklin's advisory board; and the grand jury's report cleared by name the men its foreman's social-professional world touched.

## CHAPTER 8 — The Money Trail

Follow the money was Senator Schmit's instinct, and the money has a documented trail — which dead-ends in a way that is itself documented.

**The captured tracer.** The NCUA hired, in early 1989, a two-man consulting firm called Financial Advisory Group of Council Bluffs, Iowa — John Queen and Robert Kirchner — both veterans of the state-appointed apparatus that had liquidated Commonwealth Savings, Nebraska's other collapsed thrift of the era. The committee's own investigator vouched for them. In August 1990, while the Franklin audit was underway, Queen's chief assistant at the Commonwealth receivership, Kent Johnson — the former Commonwealth comptroller and the state's star witness in the Commonwealth prosecutions — was arrested by the FBI for stealing over $1 million from the receivership; Queen's signature was on every check, and he claimed forgery. Johnson simultaneously worked the Franklin audit and a company Queen had built from the bankrupt remains of Globe Neon — in which Larry King himself had held a $50,000 share before it folded. The money tracer had a documented financial overlap with the man whose money he was tracing. FAG's conclusion, delivered to the government: King "spent nearly all of it" on flowers, limousines, gold watches, and an extravagant lifestyle. Whether any portion of the $39 million went anywhere else was never publicly established. [Grade A: the Johnson arrest and roles; Grade B: the Globe Neon overlap and the "lifestyle burn" conclusion, from DeCamp's record and contemporaneous reporting.]

**The settlements.** In October 1994, through a Freedom of Information Act request, the settlements surfaced: FirsTier Bank — Franklin's correspondent bank, through whose account number 153-7-353 every dollar had flowed — paid $3 million; two Omaha law firms that had represented both King and the credit union paid $6 million — among their partners, former Governor Charles Thone. **How the fraud worked, in plain terms.** A credit union takes deposits and lends them out; the difference in interest is its margin; a federal examiner audits the books to make sure the deposits are really there. Franklin sold certificates of deposit — promises to pay, with interest, at a future date — and simply did not record many of them. The money came in; the promise was never entered in the books; the examiner saw a solvent institution. Concealing the unrecorded promises required, in the NCUA's own words, "a separate, elaborate computer program" — a second set of books, maintained in parallel, for years. The unrecorded money paid for King: the limousines and the jets and the flowers and the political life. When a CD matured, the credit union paid it — often with above-market interest, a bribe for silence — from new money, in the manner of every Ponzi scheme, named for the 1920s fraudster who paid old investors with new investors' money. What finally broke it was arithmetic: too many maturing promises, not enough new deposits, and a whistleblower the system had discarded four years earlier. [Grade A: the NCUA report; the plain-terms framing is the file's.]

**The uncounted.** The NCUA's report records that roughly $3 million in uninsured and unclaimed deposits were never repaid — the depositors, many of them the north Omaha families the credit union was founded to serve, left outside the settlement wall the lawyers and bankers built for themselves. [Grade A: the NCUA report.]

## CHAPTER 10 — The Cover-Up Question

Did anyone cover anything up? The honest answer is that the record contains eleven documented methods, each with a Grade A or B anchor, and that each also has an innocent explanation available on the record. This chapter presents both, side by side, because that is what the record supports.

**Method one: the stand-down.** In mid-1988, Omaha police officers running a child-pornography investigation touching King were told by a lieutenant that a federal agency investigating King wanted them to slow down or back off so as not to hamper the federal case; Officer Irl Carmean memorialized the instruction in a December 1988 memo. Innocent reading: task forces routinely sequence investigations, and the federal embezzlement probe was real. Critical reading: the local abuse investigation was the one thread that could have unraveled everything early, and it was strangled in its crib — the grand jury itself later noted that "no meaningful follow-up" followed reports of possible homicides. [Grade A/B.]

**Method two: declaring the case empty before examining it.** The FBI's Omaha chief announced in February 1989 that the allegations lacked substance — before his agents had interviewed King or the principal witnesses. Innocent: an early judgment based on initial soundings. Critical: the same agency then shaped every subsequent proceeding, from the state grand jury's information flow to the federal one. [Grade A/B.]

**Method three: the money-only mandate.** The U.S. Attorney's office told state officials the federal investigation was "confined to the money issues." Innocent: jurisdictional allocation, and the Mann Act — the federal law against transporting people across state lines for sex — did belong partly to federal authorities — the Chicago Tribune documented contemporaneously that the FBI was examining whether embezzled funds were used to transport minors. Critical: the one mandate that guaranteed no abuse prosecution could emerge from the federal side. [Grade A.]

**Method four: the witness machinery.** Documented names: Steve Solburg, a prosecution witness, admitted under cross-examination that the FBI had intimidated him; Shawnetta Moore and others were, in the record of the attorney general's correspondence, "reputedly intimidated"; the FBI arranged and taped a March 1990 phone call from Boner to Owen in prison — an apparent attempt to catch her in a contradiction that instead produced a terrified "Alisha, I'm scared," which Owen took as proof Boner was under a gun or a payoff; the FBI interviewed her repeatedly in solitary attempting to secure a recantation. Innocent: vigorous, even aggressive, interrogation is standard; guilty people do recant. Critical: every instrument was pointed at the accusers and none at the accused. [Grade A/B.]

**Method five: the entrapment-shaped interview and the missing phone call.** The Boner recantation depended on a phone call from Owen that the phone records show never happened; the FBI's own polygraph of Boner passed while he maintained the recantation story; the leak that he had failed ran in the press anyway. [Grade B.]

**Method six: directing non-cooperation.** The NCUA's counsel, Robert Fenner — the same man who later fed DeCamp the secret account details that advanced the money story — recorded that the FBI told him not to cooperate with Caradori. The committee tendered its concerns to the Justice Department and received no response. [Grade B.]

**Method seven: evidence custody.** The FBI took the committee's tapes in December 1989; a television station's reporter told Owen the station bought its copies from FBI agents; in the summer of 1990 the Bureau reviewed the legislature's committee files without the chairman's permission and items later went missing; within forty-eight hours of Caradori's crash, the FBI impounded all his records and subpoenaed his widow. Innocent: the Bureau was the lead agency and evidence custody was its job. Critical: the same agency whose agents were accused of coercion held every thread of evidence. [Grade A/B.]

**Method eight: the captured tracer.** The FAG arrangement — a compromised two-man shop with an embezzlement arrest mid-audit, a Globe Neon overlap with King, and a "lifestyle burn" conclusion that closed the destination question. Innocent: small firms get federal contracts; the lifestyle spending was real and enormous. Critical: the single most important forensic question of the case was answered by the least independent possible hands, and the answer foreclosed every other question. [Grade A/B.]

**Method nine: the same apparatus for both collapses.** Commonwealth and Franklin — Nebraska's twin thrift disasters — shared personnel at every level: the Franklin grand jury's first prosecutor had prosecuted the Commonwealth attorney general; the Franklin committee's investigator came from the Commonwealth records room; the Franklin money-tracers came from the Commonwealth receivership; and the World-Herald publisher's family held a stake in a Kerrey venture. Innocent: Nebraska is a small state and thrift-crime specialists are rare. Critical: every new investigation was staffed by people with institutional reasons to keep the past buried. [Grade B.]

**Method ten: the captured jury room.** The prosecutors co-authored the "independent" citizen report — the foreman admitted "one-eighteenth of the writing" — and a federal judge later held, in a published opinion, that this had no historical or common-law immunity basis, though a majority of his panel immunized it under the advisor rationale. The foreman's employer, Union Pacific, was among Franklin's biggest corporate backers, and another UP official sat on Franklin's advisory board. Innocent: prosecutors advise juries; railroad lawyers serve as citizens. Critical: the verdict's architecture was structurally conflicted, and the structural conflicts were never disclosed to the public. [Grade A: the Heaney opinion; Grade B: the conflicts.]

**Method eleven: the editorial capture.** The World-Herald — whose publisher chaired Franklin's advisory board and raised its renovation money — editorialized against the accusers at every stage, ran the polygraph-failure leak, front-paged criticism of Caradori one day after his death, called the Franklin Committee "a disgrace to Nebraska," and, in October 1991, published a scathing editorial against parole for Bonacci — after which the Parole Board reversed its unanimous vote. Innocent: the paper covered the state's largest scandal and its editorial page is independent of its publisher's board seats. Critical: no reader was ever told, in those editorials, whose name was on the board list. The paper's own editor, asked years later, said the paper had run seven hundred stories and seven thousand reporter-hours and "never did confirm" the procurement claims — the institutional answer of a monopoly that never had to. [Grade A.]

**And the one that cannot be explained either way:** the committee's counsel was warned that Caradori would be framed as the hoax's author — "get out before you lose your license" — and Caradori's own notes record that the FBI hinted it would go after him and the tape leaker for "fabricating" the investigation if Owen changed her story. [Grade B.]

## CHAPTER 15 — Where They Are Now

Every previous chapter of this document ends before the present tense. This one begins in it. The question the whole file has circled — what about the victims who are victims currently, this minute — deserves its own chapter, built on current-year data with the same grading discipline as everything before it. What follows is the landscape as documented in 2024-2026: where the children are, what the systems that touch them record, and where the records themselves fail. [Grade A unless noted.]

### 15.1 The foster-care baseline

The pipeline's supply node is measurable today, and the measurement is stark. In 2025 the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children received 23,348 reports of children missing from foster care, and assessed 17 percent of those children as likely victims of sex trafficking — 4,215 children in a single year, in the "missing from welfare" category alone. Seventy-eight percent of all missing children in the country come from foster or state care. One in seven of the 32,000-plus missing-child cases NCMEC handled in 2025 was assessed as a likely trafficking victim. The demographics narrow the picture further: 84 percent of the likely victims were fifteen to seventeen; 92 percent were girls, with the 8 percent of boys formally labeled "underidentified" by the agency itself.

Two studies give the baseline its shape. The Florida administrative study of roughly 37,000 foster youth — the largest cohort analysis in the record — found that only 7 percent ever had a trafficking allegation recorded, but 37 percent of those had run away ten or more times before the system wrote down its first allegation, and 70 percent of first allegations occurred during a runaway episode. The record trails the reality by years and by flights. The Maryland study of 2024 found that 76 percent of identified youth trafficking victims had prior child-welfare involvement, 58 percent within the twelve months before identification. The pipeline the Webbs' foster home documented in 1985 is the entry point of a system whose own data now measures the delay between the first run and the first report.

### 15.2 The online layer

The recruitment environment has moved, and the moved environment is documented with precision. Child sex trafficking reports to NCMEC's CyberTipline reached 113,530 in 2025 — up 323 percent, a surge driven partly by the REPORT Act's new mandate that platforms report trafficking and enticement, and partly by growth that is real regardless of the mandate: in the first half of 2025 alone, 62,891 such reports arrived against 5,976 in the prior year's first half. Online enticement reports totaled 1.4 million. Financial sextortion — the coercion of minors for money — ran at 137 reported incidents a day, up 37 percent, with more than three dozen teenage boys' suicides linked to it since 2021. Reports of sadistic online exploitation — violent groups on gaming and chat platforms coercing children into self-harm and filmed degradation — rose 125 percent. NCMEC's 2025 data documents three patterns that matter to every investigator: buyers approaching children directly for paid sex without any third-party trafficker; the use of abuse imagery as a "preview" that escalates to in-person exploitation; and coordinated offender groups exploiting children collectively online.

The platform concentration is the mapping finding. Five companies carry more than three-quarters of all CyberTipline reports, and only about three hundred companies report at all. Eight companies, the same oversight release found, account for 81 percent of the entire reporting stream. In September 2026, Senator Grassley's oversight release put names and failure modes on the record. Amazon's AI systems generated 1.1 million reports, of which zero contained actionable information — the company's architecture "intentionally designed not to collect or retain" identifying data. TikTok's reports flooded with unrelated content. Snapchat's reports were more than 80 percent inactionable. And Roblox was identified as the primary location where the sadistic groups meet and recruit children, with what NCMEC called an "extremely low" reporting ratio against publicly documented exploitation on the platform. NCMEC has publicly named twelve companies for inadequate reporting. The venue node of Chapter 13's map now has named, documented occupants, and a live federal bill — the James T. Woods Act, advanced by the Judiciary Committee in February 2026 — aimed at them.

And the artificial-intelligence layer demands its own honest paragraph. The countermeasure infrastructure is growing — NCMEC's Take It Down tool has processed 130,000 requests flagging a quarter-million images across thirty-six languages. Reports tagged with AI-generated imagery exceeded 400,000 in 2025; the Internet Watch Foundation logged 3,443 AI-generated videos against 13 the year before, 65 percent of them in its most severe category. Dissected, the headline number is partly artifact — 1.1 million of those reports were Amazon's bulk training-data scans containing nothing actionable — but the genuine residue, some 182,000 reports, carries a crisis of its own: perceptual hashing, the detection technology that finds known abuse images, is a mugshot book, blind to anything never seen before. Every synthetic image must therefore be investigated as if it depicts a real child, and the victim-identification capacity that should be finding children in active abuse is consumed by synthetic volume. The documentation layer's flood quietly starves the rescue layer's core function. That is the documented shape of the threat as of this writing.

### 15.3 The exit window

The pipeline does not end at identification; it ends, if it ends, years later, and the years are documented. The aging-out cohort — the twenty thousand or so young people who leave foster care each year without permanency — carries a measurable curve. Twenty percent are homeless the day they age out. Within four years, one in four has been homeless. Thirty-one to forty-six percent will have been homeless by twenty-six. Two-thirds of their post-exit homelessness arrives in the first twelve months. On the criminalization side: more than 30 percent have spent time in a correctional facility by seventeen; more than 40 percent are incarcerated by twenty; 18 percent of all American prisoners have a foster-care history. Washington's linked-data study gives the lever and its neglect: 20 percent of the exit cohort were arrested within a year against 6 percent of peers — but only 7 percent of those who took extended foster care, against 22 percent of those who did not, and extended care reaches only about 15 percent of the eligible. Each additional year in care measurably reduces incarceration, disconnection, early parenthood, and homelessness; the documented protection exists and goes mostly unused.

The trafficking linkage closes the loop. Roughly three-quarters of trafficking survivors report foster-care involvement. Sixty-nine percent ran away. Homelessness is the documented on-ramp: 64 percent of survivors were homeless or unstably housed at the moment of recruitment. And the re-trafficking window — the International Organization for Migration's ten-year study puts the highest vulnerability in the period after exit, en route to assistance, with many survivors re-exploited within two years, some returning to a trafficker rather than be homeless — means the vulnerability the document calls Cycle D does not end at rescue. It extends across the entire recovery curve when the recovery infrastructure is ninety-day shelters and a mental-health system that meets a third of the need. The economic long tail is primary data: 70 percent of survivors earn under $25,000 at exit, and 29 percent still do more than seven years later.

### 15.4 The largest node: unaccompanied children

The largest child-placement program in the country's history is also its largest documented trafficking node, and its record is current. Between 2021 and 2024, roughly 479,000 unaccompanied children were referred to the federal Office of Refugee Resettlement; more than 430,000 were released to sponsors. The Department of Homeland Security's inspector general reported that the government could not contact 291,000 of them. The vetting record, as documented in the agency's own October 2026 memorandum: in fiscal 2024 the office denied less than 1 percent of sponsorship applications, while fraud reports filed in the following year showed an intentional-documentation fraud rate of roughly 77 percent. Six fraud types are catalogued in the memo itself — false identity documents, manipulated photographs, unverifiable addresses, fingerprint fraud, undisclosed household members, age misrepresentation.

The cases give the numbers their weight, and they are documented case by case. In August 2023 a fifteen-year-old was released to a man claiming to be her biological brother; no fingerprints or DNA were requested; the sponsor was later charged with raping and impregnating her. In July 2024 a seventeen-year-old was released to a "stepsister" with unverifiable documents; that October she was found dead in a car, beside an unconscious thirty-year-old man. The clinical layer completes the picture: unaccompanied children are tested for sexually transmitted infections only if they disclose abuse, and disclosure to an unfamiliar clinician within forty-eight hours of arrival is, in the research record, unlikely — the systematic underdiagnosis is designed in. Journalists who interviewed sixty caseworkers found two-thirds of released children working full-time rather than in school, in meat plants and food packaging across half the states. And the border node connects at the case level: the man who arranged a fifteen-year-old's crossing to continue a sexual relationship that began when she was thirteen, his mother signing as sponsor. The smuggler and the sponsor are, at documented scale, the same people.

The 2025-26 response is as instructive as the failure. A backlog of 65,000 unread reports was processed into more than 4,000 investigative leads; Homeland Security Investigations made more than 500 criminal-sponsor arrests, dismantled 2,400 smuggling operations, located 145,000 children, and rescued some 1,500 from ongoing abuse. The detection gap, not the crime, was the bottleneck — proven by what happened the moment scrutiny arrived. The policy whiplash is also on record: the department created an exploitation-risk team in 2024 and removed the affirmative screening requirement in January 2025. The identification loop's policy form runs in both directions, and the file records both.

### 15.5 The institutional layer

The residential-treatment layer — the modern form of the institution that held Loretta Smith and the Uta Halee girls — was investigated by the Senate Finance Committee and reported in May 2024 under a title the committee chose deliberately: "Warehouses of Neglect." Examining four of the largest for-profit providers across five site visits, the committee documented sexual abuse of children by staff and peers as endemic to the model. A provider's own leadership told committee staff the sentence that belongs in this file next to the grand jury's "perfect cover" language: adults who want to abuse children will find their way into the child-service industry, notwithstanding every hiring screen. The record includes a staffer grooming a child with gifts and soliciting videos, youth-on-youth assaults on consecutive days, children telling visitors they preferred juvenile detention to the facility, and staff betting on residents' outcomes. The committee's remedy list — cameras, staffing investment, serious-incident reporting — is the standing-inspection agenda no child-welfare agency currently holds.

The oversight layer's own numbers complete the institutional picture. The Protection and Advocacy system — the federally funded network with legal authority to enter any facility where a person with disabilities lives — monitored nine hundred facilities in 2024 and found health-and-safety violations in 775 of them, an 86 percent violation rate. The federal child-welfare review process found very few states meeting any of its seven outcome standards. New York's comptroller documented $1.8 million paid by the city's child-welfare agency to unauthorized subcontractors without vetting evidence, with four of seven prior oversight recommendations unimplemented, while state law blocks the city's own investigative department from examining the agency. Every element is the Nebraska pattern — the captured vetting, the suppressed reporting, the oversight denied — running in the largest system in the country, in the current year.

### 15.6 The familial layer

The supply node's first ring is the family, and the modern literature documents it with the same precision the grand jury brought to the Webb home. The International Organization for Migration's multi-agency data puts family or caregiver facilitation at 41 percent of child trafficking — up to four times the adult rate — with boys at 61 percent against girls' 46 percent. The largest practitioner study's perpetrator table reads like the case file: biological mothers at 60.3 percent, stepfathers and mothers' boyfriends at 40 percent, both parents together in a fifth of cases, generational prostitution in more than half. Drugs are the currency in four of five mother-assisted cases. And the misidentification dynamic the Franklin witnesses lived through is now quantified. Nearly 80 percent of surveyed justice professionals have encountered a juvenile arrested for commercial sex whose exploiter turned out to be a family member. The children point culpability elsewhere or incriminate themselves, and three-quarters of professionals have no training on the pattern. Caregivers appear as traffickers in only a fraction of prosecuted cases — making familial trafficking the funnel's most under-prosecuted category. The Webb home was not an anomaly of the system. The literature says it was the system working as designed.

### 15.7 The corporate labor layer

The labor-demand layer is documented with names and penalties. Packers Sanitation Services — Blackstone-owned, rebranded Fortrex after the fines — employed 102 children across eight states, and its own systems flagged the minors while they worked. The penalty came to $15,138 per child, which economists consulted called "woefully insufficient." The pattern replicated across the industry — 736 Labor Department investigations covering more than 4,000 children in a single fiscal year, with $15.1 million in penalties, and a sixteen-year-old killed at a poultry plant — and the conversion gap is the finding: the Department of Homeland Security investigated whether those children, mostly unaccompanied minors, were trafficked into the plants, and criminal liability for employers remains, in the documented record, rare. The supply chain's audit layer reaches only tier one, and the due-diligence framework now spreading through law reaches the same limit.

### 15.8 The tribal and rural gaps

The measurement failures have their own documented geography. Approximately 5,700 Native girls are reported missing annually; Native women are identified as trafficking victims at rates vastly disproportionate to population; three-quarters of missing Native females are children. The jurisdictional Bermuda Triangle — tribal, federal, county, and state authority overlapping until no agency can establish venue — is documented case by case, including one in which a named perpetrator was never prosecuted because no one could establish whose jurisdiction held the shed. Arizona's child-welfare data system excludes tribal nations entirely; a federal data purge in 2025 removed the report tracking the crisis; and ninety-five percent of identified cases receive no mainstream coverage. The rural dynamics mirror the urban ones with less light: the studies are fewer, the training scarcer, and the "this doesn't happen here" assumption documented as the first barrier.

### 15.9 The scale check

The chapter's arithmetic, held honestly: one hotline identified 21,457 victims in 2025; NCMEC assessed thousands more among missing children; the FBI logged 3,702 trafficking incidents; the Department of Health and Human Services served 1,500 trafficking victims of all types. Detection grows every year; rescue does not. The 323 percent surge, triangulated against the steadier hotline and enforcement baselines, is the shape of a system whose front end works better every year and whose back end has not moved — The immigration-relief valves tell the same shape story: more than fifteen thousand applications for trafficking visas in fiscal 2024, under four thousand approved. And the children in the middle of that shape are the subject of this chapter, present tense, as of this writing.

## CHAPTER 16 — The Help: What Exists, What Works, What Fails

If Chapter 15 is where the children are, this chapter is what meets them there — the infrastructure built to find them, protect them, and put their lives back together, graded with the same honesty as everything in this file. The honest headline: the detection layer grows stronger every year and the service layer does not, and the gap between them is where the chapter's work lies.

### 16.1 The detection layer

The validated instruments exist and are barely deployed. The Greenbaum six-question screen — two positive responses flag a likely victim, with sensitivity between 80 and 92 percent across five validation studies — is the literature's standard, deployed in pediatric emergency departments and children's advocacy centers. Yet of twenty-six screening tools catalogued in a 2025 scoping review, only eight have any validation at all. The protocol that preserves testimony is equally established and equally scarce: the NICHD investigative interview protocol — the structured, research-built method for interviewing child witnesses, named for the federal institute that developed it, whose meta-analytic record shows invitations rising 1.6 standard deviations, suggestive prompts falling 0.63, and central details rising 0.9 — with the training finding that defines the whole layer: one-shot training produces no durable change; only distributed training with ongoing video-review feedback does. The Franklin committee had no protocol, no trained interviewers, and no feedback loop; the modern record shows what that absence costs and what the presence requires.

The rest of the detection architecture carries the same shape. The school system — the one institution touching nearly every child — trains fewer than forty percent of educators on trafficking, with a quarter of those trained unsure how to report, and only three states mandate the curriculum. The healthcare layer's clinical rules are documented and humane: aggressive pursuit of disclosure is harmful and diminishes the likelihood a child will ever tell; one trained interviewer, minimal retellings, and the boundary that the Franklin system never observed — mandated reporters, not mandated interveners; immediate removal is not always safety. Fifty-four percent of hospitals have no trafficking response program at all. The hotline machinery works — consent-based, two hundred languages, a documented referral topology — and its last mile is honestly unevaluated: no published study measures how many calls become engaged services. The conversion gap, like the detection gap, is named here rather than hidden.

### 16.2 The law-enforcement layer

The national survey gives the layer's numbers: 97.5 percent of agencies claim at least one victim-centered practice, but fewer than 10 percent have a specialized child-trafficking multidisciplinary team — the coordinated unit of investigators, prosecutors, and service providers that the research finds predicts whether cases get investigated — and the smallest departments, which cover most of America's geography, sit at six percent. Victim advocates are nearly universal; the infrastructure that actually predicts investigations — specialized task forces plus victim-service collaboration — is the exception. The Dallas model stands as the documented counter-pattern: twice-monthly operations to find runaways and exploited youth, trauma-informed interviewing, placement in staff-secure shelter rather than detention, and the explicit recognition that running away leads to trafficking. Texas now requires specialized trauma-informed training for every juvenile corrections officer in the state.

The chapter's sharpest finding belongs to this layer, because it quantifies the Franklin inversion. When a child's case begins with police-initiated action, the child is almost eight times more likely to be processed as a delinquent than when the case begins with a report from the child, family, or a provider. Who finds the child determines what the child becomes — the same mechanism that turned the Franklin witnesses into defendants, measured three decades later. And the custodial form of the same barrier is documented at the federal level: as of September 2025, exactly zero cases had been filed under the 2022 statute criminalizing sexual acts by federal officers against people in custody, The named cases give the barrier its texture: Baltimore's consolidated child-victim suits allege decades of abuse of children in police custody; a Loveland, Colorado officer was convicted in 2025 of on-duty sexual abuse of a teenager; and the Department of Justice paid $138.7 million to survivors of the gymnastics doctor after the Federal Bureau of Investigation's own inaction, the institutional-failure liability benchmark. And the Justice Department's own explanation is on record — victims are reluctant to report the police to the police. Japan's 2024 record — 416 child-prostitution referrals while formally declining to identify the children as trafficking victims — shows the same loop as a policy choice in cross-national comparison. And Canada's documented experience shows the correction works: change the reporting landscape and disclosures rise. The non-punishment principle — that victims must not be prosecuted for crimes their exploitation compelled — is the design rule all of this serves, with the documented complication that some victims transition into recruiting roles, which the framework addresses through prosecutorial discretion rather than blanket immunity.

### 16.3 The services layer

The inventory is measured, and the measurement is the chapter's case. Of screened providers nationwide, 74 percent offer emergency-only shelter, 18 percent transitional, 4.5 percent residential, and 1.36 percent long-term — with long-term the rarest category and the most successful. The standard shelter stay is capped at fifteen, thirty, or ninety days, and the practitioners' own verdict is quoted in the research: "I can't stabilize a client with an extensive trauma history within ninety days." Housing is ranked the number one barrier by professionals across disciplines; survivors exit trafficking with no safe place, and the documented cycle sends them back. Trauma-informed mental health — needed by 75 percent at exit — is still unmet for 39 percent at follow-up. The faith-based layer holds much of the housing inventory precisely because public funding's time limits push providers out of the system, trading stability for freedom; its shelter leadership claims six of every ten trafficking beds in the country — a self-reported figure, logged as such, but consistent with the inventory split — and it serves the populations secular agencies turn away, including male survivors, whom one study found served by just one organization of fourteen. The evaluation culture across all of it is thin to absent — the Government Accountability Office's finding that anti-trafficking interventions are unevaluated has stood, unrevised, for two decades.

### 16.4 The legal-remedy layer

The remedies now exist that would not have existed in 1991, and their uneven coverage measures how much of the loop remains. The Trafficking Survivors Relief Act, signed December 2025, provides federal vacatur of convictions committed as a direct result of trafficking, with sealed filings and court reporting. Thirty states plus the District of Columbia non-criminalize child victims fully; eighteen protect them from collateral crimes; nineteen still permit child prostitution charges despite the federal victim definition. One practitioner organization reports 89 percent of its convicted trafficking clients are Black, Indigenous, or people of color. The civil track is the documented counter to the settlement wall: more than eleven hundred Trafficking Victims Protection Act cases, a fourteen-million-dollar jury award on record, and reach to third-party enablers — anyone who knowingly benefited. But the collection problem is the Bonacci judgment's story told at scale: restitution is ordered in only about 68 percent of convictions and often unpaid, and the practitioner's doctrine is on record — for the trafficking victim, winning is collecting. Israel's model, restitution as the default with the court required to explain any failure, stands as the documented best practice.

The silence contracts are losing ground, slowly and on record. Trey's Law, introduced in March 2026 with bipartisan sponsors, would void contractual provisions suppressing disclosure of child sexual abuse; The parallel ledgers give the silence contracts their scale: twenty years and more than $5 billion in clergy-abuse settlements, thirty dioceses bankrupted, a $2.46 billion Boy Scouts trust, and a four-billion-dollar county foster-care settlement covering sixty-eight hundred claims back to 1959 — with 68 percent of Church allegations arriving through attorneys, proof that lookback windows, not institutional conscience, drive disclosure. Missouri has already voided existing abuse nondisclosure agreements outright; the United Kingdom voided both employment-settlement and victim-settlement silences by statute. Against this, the institutional playbook stands documented: privilege-shielded internal investigations, the quiet reassignment of abusers the field calls passing the trash, willful ignorance calibrated to avoid the mandatory-reporting trigger, and litigation-public-relations coordination that treats transparency as a coverage problem. The demand side's own evidence is logged honestly alongside: the john-school programs modeled on San Francisco's celebrated model carry a contested evaluation record — the flagship study's recidivism cut met point-for-point by a methodological critique finding no effect — while the buyer-side documentation is firmer: a matched-sample study records buyers' own deterrent calculus, in which three days in jail deters seventy-one percent, a month deters all of them, and public exposure tops every list. The demand side's operational test came in the summer of 2026, when the World Cup arrived. FinCEN issued its trafficking advisory and key-term reporting directive; a White House task force had stood since March 2025; and the documented result, announced by the Department of Homeland Security on July 29, 2026, was 905 arrests and 180 victims rescued — 150 adults and 30 children. New Jersey's operation alone recovered 97 suspected victims and made 71 arrests, of which 40 were alleged buyers, and a prosecutor on a 24-hour rotation in Houston stated the modern financial doctrine in one sentence: get to their payment app, because the payment app makes the case. The honest analytical caveat stands beside the numbers — the evidence that mass events create surges is mixed; they intensify existing conditions — which is why the permanent-task-force model, in the documented best practice, does not wait for events.

The 1990 forms — sealed warrants, settlement confidentiality, the monopoly editorial — now have names, statutes, and a federal bill.

### 16.5 The recovery layer

The recovery curve has its window and its economics, both documented. The two years after exit carry the highest re-trafficking risk, with the journey to assistance the single most vulnerable interval; survivors return to traffickers rather than face homelessness; the trauma bond and the economic reality pull together. The needs at exit, ranked by survivors themselves, are trauma-informed mental health, trustworthy people, a safe place, and a living wage — in that order of voice, though not of funding. And the movement that knows all this best is now insisting on leading itself: Polaris appointed a survivor, Megan Lundstrom, as its chief executive in 2025; the movement's internal literature names the extractive pattern — inclusion limited to retelling trauma for fundraising — and survivor leaders state, in their own documented words, that most survivors do not desire prosecution, because criminalization was part of their trafficking. That sentence, from the people the whole apparatus claims to serve, is the chapter's most important datum, and it belongs in every design decision the next chapters describe.

## CHAPTER 17 — The Map: How to Read a Trafficking Network

Chapter 13 mapped the pattern across a century; this chapter is the working manual — how to build such a map today, with documented methods, documented sources, and documented cautions. The principle that organizes everything comes from the network science and was proven by the succession record: map the functions, not the faces. Operators are replaceable — the file's own lineage, from the island to the island, is the empirical proof — but every function requires an institutional host, and institutional hosts are stable, public, and investigable.

### 17.1 The principle, proven

Social-network analysis research converges on exactly what the Franklin lead list found by hand. In dismantling simulations, removing the most-connected or the most peripheral actors fails; the effective target is the moderately connected brokers — the scouts, arrangers, and transporters who move people, money, and access between the center and the edge. Cornell's network-generator work formalizes the resilience the other way: traffickers respond to disruption by recruiting new victims, which is why interdiction models that ignore restructuring mislead. The mapping deliverable, then, is a matrix of functions to hosts to names to evidence to confidence — the lead list modernized, with the investigator's own confidence annotated at every edge, exactly as Caradori annotated his.

### 17.2 The four layers and their sources

Each layer of the architecture now has standing public sources, and the investigator's discipline is knowing them.

*The institutional layer* — the supply node's hosts — lives in licensing registries, state contracting records, comptroller audits, and inspector-general reports. The Nebraska normalcy plans, the New York subcontractor audit, and the Senate Finance Committee's provider report are the documented demonstrations: every institution that holds children under contract produces a paper trail, and the paper trail is public.

*The money layer* is already collected; the gap is access. FinCEN receives 4.7 million suspicious-activity reports a year, including monthly batches identifying purchasers and facilitators of child exploitation, with a documented key-term system — trafficking reports carry a specific identifier, and victims are never named as subjects. The safe-harbor channel for institutions to share intelligence with each other exists. The indicator sets are public: frequent payment-app transfers with vague memos, funnel-account geography, advertising financed by traceable small payments, the front-business typology that names modeling agencies and matchmaking services; and the validated grooming model — five stages from victim selection through trust, desensitization, and post-abuse maintenance, with a victim-selection profile (non-nuclear families, poor supervision, feeling unloved) that matches the foster-care population point for point, and prevalence above ninety percent in abuse — the operational signatures that identify recruitment in progress, accounts held in victims' names, the lifestyle mismatch the money tells against the life. The enforcement precedent stands beside the catalogs: TD Bank paid $1.3 billion in 2024 after regulators found 98 percent of its domestic transfers effectively unmonitored, the payment apps lumped into codes that could not see them. Canada's financial-intelligence unit shows what proactive disclosure looks like — two-thirds of its trafficking disclosures name networks the police did not know. The enforcement precedent stands beside the catalogs: TD Bank paid $1.3 billion in 2024 after regulators found its systems had left 98 percent of domestic transfers effectively unmonitored, with the payment apps lumped into codes that could not see them. The Wyden method, in one sentence: follow the settlement wall, because the settlements mark where the truth was priced.

*The venue layer* has a live docket: platform filings and reporting ratios, state age-verification statutes and their litigation, the congressional naming of the twelve inadequate reporters, and the international wave — Australia's under-sixteen ban, the European directive due this year. The venue map is no longer a research project; it is a reading assignment.

*The suppression layer* — the protection functions — is documented in court dockets, settlement records with confidentiality provisions, bankruptcy filings timed against lookback windows, the NDA statutes and Trey's Law, editorial-ownership records, and the oversight-opinion fights. The structural-silence mechanism of Chapter 13 has its current form here, and every edge of it is public.

### 17.3 The discipline

Three disciplines separate a map from a conspiracy chart. First, annotate confidence as you go; the lead list's power was its honesty about what was checked and what was not. Second, record the machine as written and as run — the ORR policy guide describes a vetting apparatus, and the failure record shows the checks skipped; the distance between text and run is the map's most important edge, and it is documentable at both levels. Third, hash everything; the documented modern form of evidence tampering is the post-release modification of released files, caught only by independent hash-audit, and a map built on unverifiable files is a map that will not survive contact with the settlement wall.

### 17.4 The case studies

Four demonstrations, graded throughout. The UAC node: mapped from the ORR memo's fraud typologies, the inspector-general loss figures, the case-level sponsor-smuggler record, and the backlog-to-rescue sequence — the largest node, fully documented, public. ### 17.5 The honest limit

The map's last caution is the data substrate itself. The federal child-welfare dataset runs two years late — its most recent complete year counted 328,947 children in care on a single day — and two states did not submit at all, the recent rebuild broke year-over-year comparability, and the diversion track that erases dispositions is itself a documented design, with the evaluation literature measuring its effect — alternative-response programs produced a 6 to 36 percent decrease in identified victims where adopted, because a diverted case is, by design, not entered into the system. ## CHAPTER 18 — The Design: How to Investigate Properly

The last chapter is the one the whole extension exists for: what a proper investigation looks like, designed against the documented failure catalog of the Franklin case and built from the remedy machinery that now exists. The honest framing first: some of what happened in 1988-1991 can never be recovered, and this chapter does not pretend otherwise. But the question of what is recoverable has a documented answer — and the distance between the two is exactly what good design closes.

### 18.1 The failure catalog, answered

The Franklin case produced a short list of what made truth unreachable, and each item now has a documented counter-design. The money was traced once, by captured hands; today the counter-design is independent financial tracing through financial-intelligence channels with conflict-vetting, and the lesson of the captured tracer — vet the tracer's entanglements harder than the target's — written into the engagement itself. The flight manifests did not legally exist; today the counter-design is the standing data-source inventory of Chapter 17, where the venue and transport layers produce records whether or not any single case requires them. The photographs died with the briefcase; today the counter-design is hash-audited evidence custody and the assumption that the file will be attacked. And the witnesses were prosecuted; today the counter-design is the stack described next, every element of which exists and none of which existed in 1991.

### 18.2 The remedy stack

Six layers, each documented. Find the child with a validated screen — the Greenbaum instrument and its eighty-percent-plus sensitivity. Preserve the first disclosure on video under a structured protocol — the NICHD method, with the training finding that one-shot training does not last built into the budget. Keep the testimony through the law's preservation machinery — the child-declarant and tender-years exceptions, the 2025 rulings — Oregon's State v. Akins chief among them — admitting delayed-disclosure expertise as of right, and forfeiture-by-wrongdoing where intimidation genuinely silences a witness, the doctrine the recanting-but-present witness never fit but the deaths-ledger cases would. Where a witness disappears through wrongdoing, the doctrine applies by its terms. Protect the witness from criminalization — the non-punishment principle, the safe-harbor statutes, and federal vacatur for those already convicted. And design for the witness's own stated preference — survivors' documented ranking of what they actually need, with prosecution last among the state's instincts and first among almost no survivor's.

### 18.3 The institutional models

The blueprints exist at scale, with their frailties documented beside their strengths. The United Kingdom's seven-year Independent Inquiry into Child Sexual Abuse — the IICSA — with 325 hearing days, 2.4 million pages, more than six thousand private testimonies — produced the structure the Franklin committee lacked: subpoena power, a truth-telling project, a proactive-detection mandate, and a standing child-protection authority now in consultation. It also documented its own near-failures — three chairs, a resignation, an underfunding warning — which are the design cautions: independence must be structural, not personal; resourcing must be statutory, not charitable. Australia implemented what England debated; the single-house child-interview center, where the forensic interview, the medical exam, the therapy, and the legal process happen under one roof with one recorded conversation, is the institutional form that prevents the retelling trauma the research documents. And the protection-and-advocacy system's standing access authority — the legal power to walk into any facility where a child with disabilities lives — is the inspection power no child-welfare agency holds, the standing-correction mechanism the Franklin institutions never faced.

### 18.4 The honest limits

The design chapter ends where the whole file ends: with what investigation cannot do. The documented layer is recoverable; the contested layer can be narrowed by instruments that cannot be defeated, until what remains is either facts or provable impossibilities; and the thirty-six-year distance between the two scandals exists precisely because the first case was investigated with instruments that could be defeated and the second, finally, is not entirely. Some questions will remain open after the best investigation this design can build. The children this minute are the reason the third case has to be built differently from the first one — and the record assembled in these eighteen chapters is the blueprint for building it, and the standing answer to anyone who asks whether the truth is even reachable. It is reachable to the extent the instruments are honest; that extent is now documented; and the building is the work that remains.
